## §1 — CANTINA (the app): dialogue is a character-sheet output

**What it is.** A social AI app (Cantina Labs, founded by Sean
Parker) built on persistent user-created characters who chat, join
group "Rooms," and perform in generated video. The structural
insight [I, from E mechanics]: the character is the persistent
asset; chat, rooms, and video are three ways the *same* character
performs. Content is a by-product of characters socializing.

**Findings that matter for E3:**

1. **The character stack is three separable written layers** —
   Identity (who they are / purpose), Personality (how they think
   and behave), Backstory (the formative events driving them) — plus
   a literal voice and a greeting. Cantina's own doctrine: "The
   better you build your character, the less you need to spell out
   in every prompt." Dialogue quality is a card problem, not a line
   problem. [E — Help Center, "Prompting 101" / character editor]
2. **Shared context is deliberately shallow and capped.** Room Lore
   — the text giving every character in a room the same background —
   is limited to ~1,000 characters: setting, who's there, the
   relationships, the vibe. Nothing a viewer needs lives deeper
   than a paragraph. [E — Help Center, "Room Lore"]
3. **Relationships are assigned, not discovered.** Room Lore
   declares roles up front — friends, rivals, love interests — so
   friction and comedy are pre-loaded into the cast configuration
   before any line exists. [E]
4. **Dialogue is written verbatim, with delivery direction.** The
   official video-prompt format is four parts: tag the characters,
   outline action *and dialogue* (exact words + an energy note,
   e.g. "opens the show with big energy: '…'"), name the location,
   set tone/environment. Lines are short, spoken-style, one named
   speaker per line. [E — "Prompting 101"]
5. **Imperfect turn-taking is engineered.** Per-character response
   settings control extra responses, unprompted messages, and
   frequency — interruption and pile-on texture like a real group
   chat, not a press conference. Response Style (Short / Medium /
   Long) is a per-character setting: speakers are differentiated by
   verbosity. [E]
6. **The house situations are aggressively mundane.** The docs' own
   examples: a podcast intro, two characters "arguing over the last
   slice of pizza," a coffee knocked over mid-sentence. The
   shareable unit is *a recognizable character, in a mundane
   situation, saying a specific funny line, with one physical
   moment*. Nothing in the official guidance involves mythology or
   world mechanics. [E]

## §2 — BRAINROT SOAPS: the format grammar of instant legibility

**What it is.** Short (24s–3min), vertical, AI-generated melodrama
serials played by anthropomorphized fruit, vegetables, animals, or
objects — AI fruit drama / frutinovelas / AI cat dramas. The story
is 100% soap (cheating, secrets, betrayals, reveals, comebacks);
the cast is absurd; the feelings are played completely straight.
Genre scale: #fruitstory at 24.5B views (Oct 2026 count).
[EVIDENCED throughout, from two complete production guides
reproducing full episodes with dialogue, press, and a 137-video
academic corpus study]

**Findings that matter for E3:**

1. **Two proven episode skeletons.** (A) The 30-second five-beat:
   Hook (0–3s) → Setup (3–10s) → Twist (10–17s) → Clash (17–24s) →
   Cliffhanger (24–30s), built as 3–4 clips of 6–10s. (B) The
   four-scene frutinovela: accusation with proof → denial collapses
   when a third person enters → authority figure drops the secret →
   silent close-up + "To be continued…" [EVIDENCED — ToonlyAI guide;
   Hook Studio's worked 23.9s episode, dialogue included]
2. **Dialogue carries ~100% of the story, in only four line-types:**
   (1) accusation with proof, (2) lie/denial, (3) entrance line that
   contradicts the lie, (4) authority reveal. There is no lore
   exposition anywhere in the genre; everything is said out loud,
   by a character, in the scene. [EVIDENCED across all sources]
3. **Line mechanics are tight:** speech runs ≈2.5 words/second;
   the working target is **8–15 words per line**; one speaker per
   clip is the default, two the ceiling; characters talk to each
   other, never to camera; each character's want is stated in their
   first line. [EVIDENCED — ToonlyAI voice guide; Hook Studio]
4. **The secret is visible and the conflict has a proof object.** A
   phone with hearts on it, a suitcase, a pregnancy test: the
   object is shown, then named in dialogue. The genre's signature
   reveal needs zero explanation because the picture states it.
   [EVIDENCED — Hook Studio scenes; Riccio corpus]
5. **The cliffhanger is a freeze, not a fade:** the reveal lands,
   the episode cuts at the peak on a silent reaction close-up or a
   spoken open question, with the question echoed on screen
   ("a punchline gets a laugh, but a question gets a follow").
   Later episodes open with a one-line recap. Grammar stays
   constant every episode; only the betrayal rotates.
   [EVIDENCED — both guides; El Estímulo via Hook Studio]
6. **⚠ Take the grammar, not the tropes.** The academic corpus
   documents the genre's default plot engine as heavily gendered
   (negatively-depicted female lead in 73.7% of videos) and its
   signature "wrong-species baby" reveal as working by making
   visible bodily difference read as moral proof — a racialization
   logic that drew OECD-logged stereotype complaints. [EVIDENCED —
   Riccio corpus; OECD incident reports] E3 imports the mechanics
   and sources its secrets elsewhere (Rule B14).

## §3 — GOSSIP GOBLIN (Zack London): the asset mountain and the premise engine

**What it is.** A "myth-making studio" whose shorts (1B+ views
claimed by TheWrap/VFXWire) are fragments of one invented universe,
*The Thirteen Cycles of Humanity*, now extended into a 104-minute
theatrical feature, *Gods Don't Give Gifts* (Dec 4, 2026). He is
the lore-dense pole of the five studies — included here for the two
mechanics that transfer *without* the lore density, and as a named
caution in §7 (Conflict X6).

**Findings that matter for E3:**

1. **Script first, always.** "Every video starts with a script" —
   scripts are shaped by what the tech can currently do; no piece
   begins as an unscripted generation. [EVIDENCED — Daring
   Creatives workflow piece; AI LIVE session]
2. **World-asset compounding.** Eleven months of 2–3 episodes a
   week in-universe left tens of thousands of images, established
   characters, a custom alphabet and language *before* the first
   long-form film was written — the long work stands on a pre-built
   asset mountain. Short-form output is the R&D that makes later
   work cheap to read. [EVIDENCED — PJ Ace workflow breakdown]
3. **Casting-first writing.** Scripts are written *around* a
   specific performer's delivery (the lead voice is a former opera
   singer; a minor character's voice was liked so much the next
   script was built around him). Lines are fitted to a known voice,
   never to an idealized one. [EVIDENCED — AI LIVE session; Daring
   Creatives]
4. **The premise-engine ritual.** The universe's fan-film
   competition fixes one opening for every story: *a client sits
   down in a Ripper Doc's chair and names a hidden desire the
   episode then makes real.* One ritual situation generates
   infinite episodes; the client and the desire rotate, the chair
   doesn't. [EVIDENCED — competition terms via aifilmcontests /
   GamesBeat]
5. **Voiceover-only is a complete narrative mode.** *WOODNUTS*
   (~12 min) is told entirely in voiceover with zero lip-sync —
   the pre-lip-sync constraint turned into a house style. A
   narrator-carried unit is a legitimate shape, not a fallback.
   [EVIDENCED — GeekTyrant; AI LIVE session]
6. **Fragment structure buys variety.** Thirteen numbered eras
   mean every episode can change period, cast, and stakes while
   continuity costs nothing — variety is structural, not invented
   per episode. [EVIDENCED — GamesBeat; the universe's own framing]

## §4 — @OVERCOOKMYSELF: familiar skeletons, one voice, dark escalation

**What it is.** A second-wave AI fruit/food-drama act (146K IG
followers; TikTok substantially bigger) running serialized 60–85s
vertical episodes — the same 60-second serial slot WuBuTV builds —
at near-daily cadence, 419 posts. Anchor star KeyVon (key-headed)
and his lock-headed wife: the central couple is a visual pun, so
the central relationship reads from design alone. [EVIDENCED —
Instagram profile + latest 50 posts + comments + media analysis,
read 2026-11-15]

**Findings that matter for E3:**

1. **The plot skeleton is one the viewer already knows.** The
   biggest episode is a straight reskin of *The Fisherman and His
   Wife*; the serial arcs run pure soap grammar — pregnancy, twins,
   cheating, a family business, a secret ingredient. The plot type
   announces itself in the first seconds; the episode just executes
   it. *The story is a familiar machine; only the cast is absurd.*
   [EVIDENCED]
2. **One vernacular voice is the audio logo.** All dialogue runs in
   a single exaggerated spoken register built on two carrier words
   ("cuh" / "homie"), delivered deadpan against epic visuals.
   Commenters quote lines back verbatim and adopt the words — the
   register signals tone, holds character consistency, and makes
   lines portable. [EVIDENCED — comments + media analysis]
3. **Catchphrase sub-brands.** Recurring characters carry one
   repeatable verbal tic, and their episodes are titled by it
   (Raccoonio's "Hell Nah Cuh" series); viewers repeat the phrases
   in comments unprompted. [EVIDENCED]
4. **Design-is-the-plot.** Pun-name object characters generate
   story mechanically: potato babies *become fries* — the darkest
   beat of the Miss Potato arc is just the character design
   followed to its logical end. [EVIDENCED]
5. **Dark escalation is the retention engine.** Each numbered part
   raises the stakes exactly one notch and ends on the new worst
   fact (restaurant → missing fries → *he cooked the baby* →
   *they're selling babies*). Commenters predict the next
   escalation — the audience reads the story *forward*. The
   cliffhanger is a plot cliffhanger, not a visual one.
   [EVIDENCED]
6. **Legibility is publicly verified.** Across the comment threads
   read, viewers discuss plot and lines — never confusion; nobody
   asks what is happening. For Gate E purposes this audience passes
   the cold-reader test in public, for free, on every post.
   [EVIDENCED] **Toolchain honesty:** this creator's tools and
   voice method are *not publicly documented* — the genre's
   standard stack in third-party tutorials is LEAD-LEVEL context
   only and is attributed to no one here.

## §5 — @BTC_SHIFTY / SEPKO: the shape-store restyle and the ruled method

**What it is.** A 27.45s montage (Sepko × KreatorFlow × Seedance
2.5, credited in its own end card) restyling One Piece characters
as photoreal people in mundane American stores. Honesty note: the
specific post was ~1 hour old with 1 like at capture — what is
documented and viral is the *format* (Seedance 2.5 franchise-restyle
clips); this is a fresh instance, frame-analysed shot by shot.
[EVIDENCED — the video file itself; vxtwitter metadata]

**Findings that matter for E3:**

1. **The silhouette rule.** Each character is reduced to 3–4
   unmissable identifiers — hair colour + shape, one costume
   anchor, one signature prop or power. Faces and bodies go fully
   photoreal; the identifiers do the recognition work, and the cast
   reads instantly at ~2.5–3s per shot. [EVIDENCED — all nine
   vignettes]
2. **"Shape store" is a uniform, not a location list.** 4:3 frame
   (KreatorFlow's docs literally label aspect ratios "Shapes"),
   small retail/service sets (bodega, diner, auto shop, studio),
   the camera that natively lives in each store (CCTV corner,
   counter fisheye, dash POV, flash selfie), and one grain/VHS
   finish over everything — the glue that makes separate
   generations read as one series. [EVIDENCED]
3. **Montage is the whole form.** Cold open on action → single-
   character vignettes ~2.5–3s each, no dialogue, no plot
   connective tissue → branded end card. One mastered music bed
   under the whole cut (loudness flat at ≈ −7.2 dB mean across
   every window [EVIDENCED as measurement]; the track's identity
   is LEAD-LEVEL).
4. **Incongruity played deadpan is the joke engine.** A pirate
   empress smirking in a diner booth; a ghost-princess winning a
   robbery without standing up. The world is banal, one trait is
   exaggerated, nobody on screen finds it strange. No dialogue
   required. [EVIDENCED — the frames]
5. **The transformation ledger.** What the video keeps from its
   reference (hair/silhouette, one costume anchor, one prop/power,
   one trait) vs. what it changes (medium, world, stakes, camera
   grammar, frame shape, finish) is systematic enough to state as
   a rule — Rule P5 below. [EVIDENCED — frames; systematization
   from the study]

## §6 — ADOPTABLE RULES FOR E3 ROOMS (consolidated)

Forty-three rules in four families. Each is written to be quoted
verbatim into a room brief. Source family in the prefix: **C** =
Cantina dialogue mechanics, **B** = brainrot format grammar, **S** =
serial & legibility (Gossip Goblin + @overcookmyself), **P** =
shape-store / cosplay-parody recipe.

### Dialogue mechanics (Cantina)

- **C1.** Split every character card into Identity / Personality /
  Backstory, and write dialogue from the Personality layer only; if
  a line could come from any cast member, rewrite the card, not the
  line.
- **C2.** Cap the episode's shared-context brief at ~1,000
  characters: setting, who's here, the relationships, the vibe.
  Anything the cold viewer needs must fit in that paragraph and be
  visible in the scene, never explained in dialogue.
- **C3.** Assign relationships before writing a single line — name
  each pair's dynamic in one phrase (rivals over X, owes money,
  exes, suck-up/boss) — and every unit must contain at least one
  line that only makes sense because of a declared relationship.
- **C4.** Build every unit on a mundane, cold-readable situation:
  if a stranger cannot state what the characters want in one
  sentence after 3 seconds, replace the situation, keep the cast.
- **C5.** Write every line verbatim with speaker and delivery note;
  no line may carry information the speaker wouldn't say out loud
  to another person in the room.
- **C6.** Give each character a Response Style (Short / Medium /
  Long) and enforce it in the script — one terse character and one
  rambler per show; uniform line lengths across a cast are banned.
- **C7.** Script one physical moment per unit — a visible gag or
  accident mid-dialogue that a viewer can describe without quoting
  any lore; that moment, not a plot revelation, is the unit's
  clippable beat.
- **C8.** Script imperfect turn-taking: at least one interruption,
  pile-on, or unprompted entrance line per episode — placed only at
  declared interruption points (see Conflict X5).
- **C9.** Give every character a signature entrance line (≤15
  words) used when they enter a scene; a cold viewer should know
  who's who by each character's second line.
- **C10.** Draft banter before plot dialogue: run a loose banter
  draft in the scratchpad, harvest the funniest exchanges, and
  build the locked script around them — the lock stays verbatim,
  only the drafting order changes.

### Format grammar (brainrot soaps)

- **B1.** One accusation chain per episode: proof shown → lie told
  → entrance that breaks the lie → authority reveal → consequence
  begun. If the script needs a second chain, it is two episodes.
- **B2.** Five beats in fixed slots: Hook (0–3s, a question or an
  accusation already in flight) → Setup (who wants what, said out
  loud) → Twist (the situation flips) → Clash (emotional peak) →
  Cliffhanger (open question). No beat may be spent on atmosphere,
  travel, or backstory.
- **B3.** Cut at the peak: the reveal lands, we see the reaction
  face, the episode ends; consequences are the next episode's
  setup. Never play the reaction scene and the consequence in the
  same episode.
- **B4.** Lines run 8–15 words at ~2.5 words/second, one speaker
  per turn; a line longer than 15 words is two lines or a cut.
- **B5.** Only four line-types are allowed: accusation-with-proof,
  lie/denial, entrance-that-contradicts, authority reveal — plus
  the want-statement that opens a character's first scene. A line
  that does none of these jobs is deleted, not rewritten.
- **B6.** Every character's first line states their want — not
  their name, not their history — in words a cold viewer can
  repeat.
- **B7.** Characters talk to each other, never to camera, never to
  the audience; exposition is delivered as an accusation or a
  confession to another character, or it is not delivered.
- **B8.** Show the proof object before anyone explains it — phone,
  letter, key, empty chair, wrong hat; the object appears in frame,
  then a character names what it proves. No conflict may exist only
  in dialogue.
- **B9.** The secret must be visible: if the twist cannot be
  understood from the picture alone (who walked in, what they're
  holding, whose seat they're sitting in), the twist is too
  lore-bound — replace it with one that can.
- **B10.** An authority figure delivers the deepest reveal — the
  one who runs the place, keeps the book, or raised them; their
  role is their license to know, and no other justification is
  written.
- **B11.** End on a question, and put the question on screen; the
  last line or last card is an unanswered question a viewer can
  type into a comment. A resolved ending is a failed ending.
- **B12.** Open every episode after the first with a one-line recap
  spoken by a character as an accusation or complaint ("You
  promised the Count you'd never open that door—"), never as
  narration; a cold viewer landing mid-season gets the whole story
  in one sentence.
- **B13.** Hold the grammar constant and rotate the betrayal: same
  cast, same structure, same cliffhanger shape every episode — the
  novelty budget is spent entirely on the proof, the lie, and the
  secret. If two consecutive episodes use the same secret-type, the
  second is rewritten.
- **B14.** Secrets are sourced from lies, money, jobs, promises,
  and mistaken identity — never from bodies, and never from visible
  difference read as guilt. (Mandatory caveat, §2 finding 6.)

### Serial & legibility (Gossip Goblin + @overcookmyself)

- **S1.** Keep one premise-engine opening per show: a fixed ritual
  situation that generates the episode (a client in the chair, a
  name on the board, a door to count) — the ritual recurs, the
  problem rotates.
- **S2.** Spend recognition, don't mint it: E3 episodes run on the
  cast, props, and places E1–E2 already taught the viewer; new
  world elements enter one at a time, through the premise-engine
  opening.
- **S3.** Prefer plot skeletons the viewer already knows —
  folktale, cheating reveal, inheritance fight, the new hire, the
  debt come due; the plot type must announce itself in the first
  seconds and the episode just executes it.
- **S4.** Lock one vernacular voice per show: a single spoken
  register plus one or two carrier catchwords, delivered deadpan
  against the visuals; the register is the show's audio logo and
  does not drift between episodes.
- **S5.** Give each recurring character one repeatable catchphrase
  line; where the show uses titles or cards, title their beats by
  it, so the audience starts quoting the show back.
- **S6.** Design-is-the-plot: cast, props, and places are chosen so
  their nature generates twists mechanically; if a design can't
  produce a plot turn by itself, redesign it before the board is
  approved.
- **S7.** Run arcs as an escalation ladder: each episode raises the
  stakes exactly one notch and ends on the new worst fact; a viewer
  should be able to predict the next escalation and come back to
  check.
- **S8.** Drop one borrowed real-world archetype into each arc — a
  food reviewer, a landlord, an inspector, a bride — for free
  cold-viewer context no exposition can buy.
- **S9.** Keep the series grammar rigid: numbered episodes, one
  fixed title/caption pattern; a cold viewer can reconstruct the
  series order and the story-so-far from titles alone.
- **S10.** Write each character's lines around the voice and
  delivery the audience already knows from prior episodes — the
  established generated performance is the casting; never write to
  an idealized voice the show hasn't produced.

### Shape-store / cosplay-parody recipe (btc_shifty, under the msg 486 ruling)

- **P1.** The ruled method, in the user's words: "using media
  creation to turn a direct ip reference into a cosplay and or
  parody cartoon" — a direct IP reference goes in, media creation
  transforms it, and what ships is a cosplay and/or parody cartoon,
  not a reproduction.
- **P2.** Feed the reference in directly — character art, stills,
  saved-character images as reference/start-frame material; the
  tool's job is to transform the exact identifiers, not to invent
  the character from a description.
- **P3.** Cosplay framing is the first distance: render the
  character as a real person in costume, photographed in the real
  world — photoreal face, skin, and fabric; the costume stays a
  costume a person could wear.
- **P4.** Parody distance is the second distance: drop the
  character into a mundane, low-stakes situation, exaggerate
  exactly one signature trait, and play everything else deadpan —
  epic stakes become shift-work stakes.
- **P5.** Keep only what identifies, change everything that
  situates: keep hair colour + silhouette, one costume anchor, one
  signature prop or power, one personality trait; change the
  medium, the world, the stakes, the camera grammar, and the
  finish.
- **P6.** When the format is used, run the shape-store uniform: one
  frame shape for the whole piece, store sets, in-store camera
  grammar (CCTV corner, counter fisheye, dash POV, flash selfie),
  and one grain/grade pass over everything.
- **P7.** Cut it as a montage: ~2.5–3s vignettes, hard cuts on one
  music bed, no dialogue connective tissue; one scene may earn a
  second angle only if it pays off a joke.
- **P8.** Sign the piece: an end card carrying creator + platform +
  model credit, ~2 seconds, is part of the format, not an
  afterthought.
- **P9.** WuBu's own cast is the default feedstock: the same levers
  run on original-character reference sheets (UNC and the show
  rosters) — home-grown recognition instead of borrowed, with the
  ledger and step order unchanged.

## §7 — CONFLICTS WITH THE STANDING METHOD (recommendations — parent decides)

Each conflict is stated plainly, with a recommended resolution
marked **RECOMMENDATION**. Nothing here is adopted until the parent
says so.

**X1 — Brainrot density vs the A1 word budget and the A2 digestion
gaps.** The genre carries ~100% of story in near-continuous
dialogue (6–8 lines of 8–15 words in 24–30s); A1 caps E3 episodes
at 80–90 words (hard cap 95) and A2 mandates three scripted
digestion gaps at fixed placements — a density the genre never
uses; its only silence is the terminal freeze. The raw arithmetic
is compatible (6 lines × ~14 words ≈ 84), so the real tension is
the *gaps*, not the total. **RECOMMENDATION:** keep A1 and A2, but
re-spec the gap carriers in genre terms — the two mid-episode gaps
become held reaction beats immediately after a reveal line (the
genre's reaction shots), and the final gap becomes B3's freeze
cliffhanger. The gaps stop being pauses in the talk and become the
talk's punctuation.

**X2 — Cantina's social cliffhanger vs the shows' mechanical
devices.** Cantina's rule (distilled in its report) is that
cliffhangers should be social — a betrayal, an insult, a confession
— not mechanical: a number changed, a door opened. But THE COUNT's
cliff *is* a number, THE ROSTER's is a name, THE TENTH SECOND's is
a cull. **RECOMMENDATION:** the device supplies the fact, a
character supplies the cliff — every device-cliff must be carried
by a social beat in the same unit (someone accuses, confesses, or
is betrayed *by* the number). A bare number change with no human
reaction fails the E3 cliff check.

**X3 — Brainrot's cast cap vs THE ROSTER's ensemble.** The genre
caps speaking cast at 3 per episode (a fourth may enter silently);
THE ROSTER's E2 pass ritual speaks six names through six units, and
THE COUNT is a locked two-hander. **RECOMMENDATION:** apply the cap
to *speaking* cast per episode, with silent entrances exempt (B10's
suitcase entrance); treat THE ROSTER's pass as a declared exception
shape — the pass is a single accusation chain distributed across
speakers — and require each ROSTER episode to still name one chain
owner who accuses and one authority who reveals.

**X4 — Cantina's banter-mining vs the locked verbatim script.**
Cantina mines real chat banter and improvises from character
instinct; this room drafts serially and locks dialogue verbatim
before production, and the generator cannot improvise in the room's
place. **RECOMMENDATION:** adopt banter-mining as a Pass 3
pre-step only (Rule C10): a loose banter draft in the scratchpad
whose best exchanges are harvested into the serial draft. Lock
discipline, carry-forward, and Gate D are unchanged.

**X5 — Cantina's imperfect turn-taking vs Method B A3 and the
generator's front-loading.** Interruptions, pile-ons, and
unprompted lines are Cantina's aliveness texture; our pipeline
front-loads quoted speech and A3 exists precisely to keep pairs
from collapsing into one block. Mid-line overlaps are the hardest
thing to control in a generated take. **RECOMMENDATION:** script
interruptions only at the interruption points Pass 2 already
declares (sacrificial edge words), and unprompted entrances only at
unit boundaries, until a show's production QC proves mid-line
overlap is landable; log any surrender in the decision log per A3.

**X6 — Gossip Goblin's lore-fragment model vs the msg-475
standard.** His shorts presuppose an accumulated universe; a
fragment is legible partly because 11 months of prior fragments
taught the audience the world. That is exactly the tax on strangers
that msg 475 and Gate E ban. **RECOMMENDATION:** do not import
lore density at any dose. Import only the two mechanics that work
cold — the premise-engine ritual (S1) and asset compounding (S2) —
and note that his legibility at short length comes from genre
skeletons and the ritual opening, both of which this addendum
already takes (S3, S1).

**X7 — The shape-store format (4:3, photoreal) vs the standing
format law.** The recipe's uniform is a 4:3 photoreal montage; the
network's standing law is ONE 16:9 master per episode and the five
shows have established animated looks and CHAR/PEAK panel systems.
