## 1. WRITING — every line is an action

- A line is not information; it is a move. The speaker wants something from
  the listener: to seduce, intimidate, defuse, manipulate, stall, or wound.
  If a line wants nothing, cut it.
- Tactic shifts reveal character: when blocked, does the speaker pivot to
  flattery, self-pity, threat, or silence? The pivot IS the character.
- Status negotiations run every exchange: high-status speakers use fewer
  words, longer pauses, and ask questions instead of answering them;
  low-status speakers over-explain, seek affirmation, fill silence in panic.
  A status shift mid-exchange is a scene.
- Counter-punch, don't Q&A: deflection, counter-questions, strategic
  misunderstanding, and non-sequiturs escalate friction. Polite sequential
  answers are dead air.
- Subtext engines: contradiction (say one thing, mean another), silence
  (a pause that confirms the worst fear), deflection/evasion (the dodge
  IS the answer), objects and environment (what a character touches or
  avoids says what they won't).
- 2+2: never hand the audience the answer. Give them the pieces and let
  them assemble it. What is withheld does the work.
- Every line carries a goal and a secret. No line exists to fill time.

## 2. SUBJECTS — stakes and specificity, or nothing

- A subject is bankable only if you can name, in one clause, what is at
  risk (a relationship, a job, safety, pride, the station itself) and for
  whom. No stake, no subject.
- Hooks must be SPECIFIC. Generic promises die. One concrete detail that
  could only happen at this station beats ten general ones.
- Only details that matter to the payoff survive. If removing a detail
  changes nothing, remove it.
- Tension escalates: each new detail makes the situation more intense,
  never merely longer. Slow down right before the big moment.
- The payoff must deliver on the hook's promise: a twist, a satisfying
  resolution, or an emotional hit. An anticlimax is a broken promise.
- First 1.5s must answer three questions at once: what is this, is it for
  me, is it worth my time — with a promise, an open loop, and proof of
  relevance. Cold-open into the tense middle whenever possible.

## 3. LORE — friction, not wallpaper

- The lore test: does this detail change a character's choice RIGHT NOW?
  If not, cut it or reframe it until it does. Lore that only decorates is
  world-stalling, not world-building.
- Systems generate plot: design systems that must collide (power vs.
  resources vs. custom vs. change vs. scarcity). When systems collide,
  stories write themselves.
- Show through action and dialogue, never explain. Rules emerge from what
  characters do and what they refuse to do. One vivid, unexpected detail
  beats paragraphs of exposition.
- A living world changes: power shifts, places carry the emotional residue
  of what happened in them, the past is an active player in the present.
- Canon discipline: what actually shipped is canon. Lore must stay coherent
  with shipped pieces, not with plans. Contradictions are repaired, not
  papered over.
