# HOW THEY TALK — dialogue craft mined from the studied creators' actual scripts

**Commissioned:** user order, Telegram msg 1296 (2026-11-27): "now use
the links and research to use their scripts to learn how to make
things" — issued minutes after his verdict (msg 1294) that WuBu's
show scripts "speak like not human": written-register proverbs and
plot summaries where people should be talking.

**Method:** this document quotes the *actual words* of the studied
pieces, taken from the banked transcripts in the lilypad study
folders — not impressions of them. Every specimen is verbatim from
a transcript file and tagged with its source study. Word counts,
line lengths and densities in §1 and §6 were computed from those
transcript files for this document. The question throughout is
narrow: **what do their lines do, mechanically, that ours don't?**

**The one-sentence answer:** their characters speak in *moves
addressed to a person* — asks, lies, brags, pleas, refusals — in
short contracted sentences loaded with address terms, repetitions
and self-incriminating specifics; WuBu's characters speak in
*moves addressed to the plot summary* — complete, polished,
past-tense sentences that report what happened instead of doing it
out loud. The factories' secret is not slang. It is that **the
line is a speech act first and an information carrier second**,
and they spend 30–50 % of their words on the human packaging
(address, repetition, reaction, hedge) that our word cap treats
as waste.

## 1. SENTENCE SHAPE — short, contracted, front-loaded with address

Measured across the five full transcripts (see §6 for density):

| Corpus | Lines | Mean line | Median | Lines ≤5 words | Lines with contractions |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| justblox12 "rust" (narration) | 49 | 11.0 w | 11 w | 16 % | 14 % |
| justblox12 "whistle" (1st-person narration) | 84 | 7.1 w | 5.5 w | **50 %** | 18 % |
| Boss_Bee_ "Spongy" (dialogue) | 30 | 10.8 w | 11 w | 3 % | **53 %** |
| clipcraft7 forehead-price (dialogue) | 20 | 14.9 w | 15 w | 0 % | **60 %** |
| clipcraft7 donut heir (dialogue) | 18 | 11.2 w | 11.5 w | 22 % | 44 % |

Three shape facts:

**(a) Dialogue corpora contract; narration corpora barely do.**
Real dialogue in these factories runs 44–60 % contraction rate
("I'ma", "gonna", "don't", "you're"); even the flat TTS narration
of justblox12 uses them for its human moments. A WuBu line with
no contraction reads as written prose because English speakers
contract almost every auxiliary they say. Specimens:

> "Sir, you mind if I box this up and take it home for my son?"
> — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy" (the episode's first ask, 2.9 s)

> "Dad, come on. You don't trust me with it?" / "Of course I
> trust you, cuh." — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy"

> "I'm sorry. I was just jealous. I thought everyone had
> forgotten about me." — clipcraft7, donut heir (the apology)

**(b) Lines start with a person, a summons, or the ask itself —
never with a topic sentence.** Openings observed: a name or
kinship address ("Sir,", "Dad,", "Yo, Unc,", "Hey, Spongy.",
"Nana,", "Grandpa,"), an attention grab ("Whoa, Dad,", "Hold
up, son.", "Wait a minute!"), or the demand naked ("Let's see
how special you are without your pickle topping" — clipcraft7,
donut heir, first line). The justblox12 narrator starts
sentences with the pivot words a storyteller actually uses:
"That's why…", "And…", "But…", "Then…", "So…". Nobody opens
with a subordinate clause or a summary ("He only grows when I
pay him. So I tried not paying." — ours, LANDLORD U3, is exactly
the shape none of them use).

**(c) Fragments are load-bearing, especially under pressure.**
Half of the whistle narrator's 84 lines are ≤5 words, and the
short ones cluster at the crisis:

> "She didn't." / "Too far." / "Only air came out." / "It was
> new." / "Not her name." / "Mine." / "Nobody spoke."
> — justblox12, whistle post (the accident and rescue)

> "Water. Cold, fresh water." / "Everyone except Ben." / "He
> hated oil." / "Splash! Ben just wiped his eyes."
> — justblox12, rust post

The Content Lab's dump scene is three fragments long and is the
whole character sheet: Cherry specifies the replacement in
**"Firm. Green. Zero spots."** (thecontentlab01, "Spotty's").

## 2. THE MOVE, SPOKEN LIKE A PERSON — begging, bragging, refusing, lying, threatening

Our law says every line must be a move. Their lines obey the
same law — the difference is the move is *performed in the
speech habits of the person making it*, so it lands as behaviour
instead of as a plot bullet. Move by move:

**Begging** — the plea is a stack of escalating offers, each one
shorter and more total than the last, ending on the smallest
possible ask:

> "Sir, please, just give me a few more days. Tack on whatever
> late fee you want. I'll pay every dime of that rent. You've
> got my word. Just don't kick us out."
> — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy" (the final 10 s; the whole episode's bill)

Five sentences, five moves: request → open-ended concession →
total promise → collateral ("my word", the phrase he already
spent at midnight: "Gave my word I'd finish the detail, ma'am")
→ the real ask, shrunk to six words. Note the plea *repeats the
stake's price in his own currency* instead of summarising his
situation. He never says "I cannot pay the rent this month" —
the summary version. He offers, offers, offers, begs.

**Bragging** — the brag carries a number and an address, and it
is answered by admiration in the same register:

> "Yo, Unc, look at this shit. Brand new jersey. Dropped 2,500
> racks on this." / "Damn, that's beautiful, squiddy boy. Real
> beautiful."
> — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy"

The brag is three fragments (summons / object / price), not a
sentence about wealth. Compare the Content Lab's vendor cry,
which is a brag as street call: "Popcorn! Fresh, crispy
popcorn!" (thecontentlab01, "Strawberina's popcorn").

**Refusing** — refusals are short, personal, and priced in the
refuser's own value-word; the grand refusal is a sentence the
other person taught the refuser:

> "Sorry, Cherry. I'm too sweet for you now." / "Bye."
> — thecontentlab01, "Spotty's" (the payoff line of a 61 s arc)

> "No, you are not on the list. Move along."
> — thecontentlab01, "Spotty's" (the club door)

> "Hold up, son. This is brand new. You just got it."
> — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy" (the boss refusing his own son's gift)

**Lying** — lies are total, casual, and *specific*; the liar
adds a helpful detail nobody asked for, which is exactly how
people lie:

> "He is out of the country on a business trip. He'll be back
> soon."
> — thecontentlab01, "Strawberina's popcorn" (the husband is
> popcorn; she is selling him)

> "Nah, little man, I already ate. This whole thing right here
> is for you, cuh. Go crazy."
> — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy" (the father's kind lie; frames show him
> not eating)

> "No, I was just checking his topping with a metal spoon
> seriously."
> — clipcraft7, donut heir (caught sabotaging his brother; the
> trailing "seriously" is the tell — liars add sincerity
> adverbs)

**Threatening / accusing** — the threat names the damage and a
price; the accusation is a question the accused must answer:

> "Eight hundred bans on this bitch and the headlight already
> fucked up? What the fuck happened?"
> — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy" (the boss; ASR "bans" = bands, $800 —
> frame captions confirm "800 / HEADLIGHT")

> "That's it — this time you're not getting away. You were
> trying to ruin his doughnut again, weren't you?"
> — clipcraft7, donut heir (ASR-merged; the tag question
> "weren't you?" forces the lie that follows)

**Confessing / apologising** — the apology names the motive the
audience suspected, in first person, without decoration:

> "I'm sorry. I was just jealous. I thought everyone had
> forgotten about me. You're still my brother. Want a piece?"
> — clipcraft7, donut heir

> "I got you, sir. I'ma check it again real careful. My bad."
> — Boss_Bee_ "Spongy" (absorbing blame for damage he may not
> have caused — apology as job preservation)

**Self-talk** — the one device none of our scripts use at all:
a character talks *himself* down, out loud, and the audience
learns his guilt from his own mouth before anyone accuses him:

> "I need to wash my face. Be normal. Act like you normally
> do."
> — thecontentlab01, "the double plot" (the captain, 0–5 s,
> clutching the briefcase that holds his plot)

## 3. REPETITION AND EMPHASIS — say it twice, name the stake again, talk to someone

**(a) The doubled phrase is the emphasis system.** No italics,
no adverbs — they just say the thing twice, or say it and then
say its consequence:

> "Damn, that's beautiful, squiddy boy. **Real beautiful.**"
> — Boss_Bee_

> "He was born worth 500,000, **just like my family**… 500,000
> at birth… This boy left the hospital with his credit already
> approved."
> — clipcraft7, forehead-price story (the number is repeated
> four times in one scene; the number IS the speech)

> "One spot turns into a hundred." → (boss, later) "You're too
> spotty. You're fired." → (payoff) "Every spot makes it a
> little extra sweet." / "I'm too sweet for you now."
> — thecontentlab01, "Spotty's" (one word — spot/sweet —
> repeated until it changes owners)

**(b) The stake is re-named inside the ask, not before it.**
Spongy's first line doesn't ask for food; it asks for food
*for my son* — the beneficiary rides inside the request. The
whistle narrator re-names the rule at the moment of use ("I
remembered the second rule. Small enough for you to carry. I
carried Evie upstairs every time she fell asleep on the sofa.")
— rule, then the receipt that proves it, in the same breath.

**(c) Address is constant.** Kinship and rank terms — "cuh",
"Unc", "little man", "sir", "ma'am", "boss", "babe", "Dad",
"son", "Grandpa", "Nana" — appear in a large share of Spongy's
30 lines, and the Boss_Bee_ study notes they "map the whole
social order without a single explanatory line." Our scripts
name characters in stage directions and then let them speak
unaddressed, like narrators. Their speakers are always talking
*to* somebody, and the word that proves it is in the line.

**(d) Direct address to the audience is a confession, not a
caption.** The whistle narrator's asides are admissions (see
§5). Gossip Goblin's corpus makes the same point from the other
side: his characters "want things, complain, haggle, and joke —
inside a world that happens to be insane", and the audience is
addressed in-world, as citizens, never lectured (ESOTERIC_STYLE
_GUIDE §1.5, §1.9).

## 4. EMOTIONAL REGISTER — emotion is volume and word choice, not description

The factories never write "angrily" into a line and never let a
character *describe* a feeling. The feeling is carried by (i)
sentence length collapsing, (ii) the value-word of the world,
and (iii) volume markers (exclamation, direct cry):

- **Panic/desperation:** "Strawberina! Help me! I am about to
  burst!" — thecontentlab01. Name first, cry second, the literal
  stakes third. Six words of setup, total.
- **Fear, reported by a third party in plain words:** "I told
  him he could leave, but he's scared as hell of losing this
  job." — Boss_Bee_ (the wife prices Spongy's fear to the boss;
  the study flags this as the beat that prices every later gift).
  Fear is *spoken about a person by someone who knows him*,
  which is how fear actually travels in life.
- **Joy/triumph:** "Wait a minute! This is actually delicious
  and so crunchy! We are going to start selling popcorn!" —
  thecontentlab01. The turn of the whole episode is a *taste
  reaction* — an involuntary human noise ("Wait a minute! This
  is actually…") that becomes a decision. Our scripts announce
  turns; theirs *react* into them.
- **Quiet threat:** "Leave that to me." — thecontentlab01,
  final line, "level and quiet" per the study. The scariest
  line in the episode is its shortest and calmest. Register
  contrast is the emphasis: peaks are exclamatory, menace is
  flat.
- **Grief, compressed:** "Oh my poor babies! How am I supposed
  to take care of you now that your dad is gone?" —
  thecontentlab01. A question to nobody, ten seconds after the
  death — then she tastes the popcorn. Grief gets one line,
  because the episode, like a person, has to keep moving.

Against this, our delivered register is uniform: every line at
the same temperature, in complete sentences, at summary
distance. Uniform temperature is the tell of written prose —
spoken people spike and drop.

## 5. NARRATION vs DIALOGUE — why justblox12's narrator sounds human and ours sounds like a summary

Both justblox12 posts are one flat TTS voice, wall-to-wall.
The rust post is third-person; the whistle post is first-person
and is the single most instructive artefact in the corpus for
us, because **WuBu's LANDLORD narration is also a first-person
voice telling his own story** — and the two voices do opposite
things with the same job.

What Kobi (the whistle narrator) does that Elias (ours) doesn't:

1. **He confesses against himself, early and on the record.**
   "Mum called me lazy because I whistled for the remote
   instead of getting up. **She was right.**" The rust study's
   sibling finding (§C.7 of the whistle STUDY): a narrator who
   reports his own failures is believed when he later reports
   the rules are satisfied. Our narrators never admit anything;
   they only report. A voice that never loses an argument with
   itself sounds like a synopsis.
2. **He admits ignorance and asks real questions.** "I could
   bring the shoe back. **Could it bring Evie with it?**" — the
   climax's key uncertainty is posed as a question he doesn't
   yet know the answer to. Summary voice knows everything in
   advance; a person inside the story doesn't.
3. **He testifies in specifics, not conclusions.** "I carried
   Evie upstairs every time she fell asleep on the sofa" —
   a domestic receipt, offered as evidence for the rule. Our
   register states the conclusion ("He only grows when I pay
   him") and skips the receipt.
4. **He reacts in real time.** "Usually into my face." (the
   teddy's nightly flight — a complaint, as a fragment, after
   the fact it describes). "For one horrible second, she swung
   upside down over the quarry." Feeling arrives *as* reaction
   fragments between facts, not as a summary after them.
5. **His reported speech keeps the other people's voices.**
   Grandpa: "This one'll reach anything you own." Mum: "Inside
   the sole. Small letters." Evie: "My teddy." — the two lines
   that carry the ending are the shortest in the piece (whistle
   STUDY §B.5). Even inside one TTS voice, quotation preserves
   each speaker's shape. Our narration paraphrases everyone
   into the narrator's own register.
6. **His sentences break where breath breaks.** 50 % of his
   lines are ≤5 words (§1). The rust narrator, third-person
   and more formal, still opens with spoken pivots — "You see,
   one drop and you start to squeak." That "You see," is two
   words of pure human packaging: it does no plot work at all,
   and it is precisely what makes the next clause sound said
   instead of written.

The concrete difference, stated flatly: **their narration is a
person remembering, out loud, in order, with opinions; ours is
a blurb — the story's moves compressed into their shortest
grammatical form, in past tense, at one temperature.** The
compression is the disease. Every device above (confession,
question, receipt, reaction fragment, quoted voice, "You see,")
is a *decompression* device: it costs words and buys a mouth.

The Gossip Goblin corpus states the same law from the
world-building side (ESOTERIC_STYLE_GUIDE §1.5): "Characters
never explain the world and never speak in riddles. They want
things, complain, haggle, and joke — inside a world that
happens to be insane." Complaining and haggling are speech
acts with a person-shaped rhythm; explaining is not.

## 6. DENSITY — the words are there; our cap is part of the problem

Measured from the banked transcripts:

| Piece | Words | Runtime | Density |
|---|---|---|---|
| justblox12, rust (narration) | 541 | 120.95 s | **268 wpm** |
| justblox12, whistle (narration) | 594 | 127.43 s | **280 wpm** |
| Boss_Bee_, "Spongy" (dialogue) | 324 | 96.9 s | **201 wpm** |
| clipcraft7, forehead-price (dialogue) | 298 | 109.2 s | 164 wpm* |
| clipcraft7, donut heir (dialogue) | 202 | 149.1 s | 81 wpm* |

\* Whole-file rates for scene-based dialogue: lines land in
bursts inside scenes, with action and reaction shots between
them, so the whole-file average understates the in-scene speech
rate. The narration figures are wall-to-wall by construction
(no silent stretch in either justblox12 piece).

WuBu's standing budget, from the LANDLORD E1 script header
itself: "Spoken load ≤ ~20 words per unit" — i.e. **≤20 words
per 10 seconds = a hard ceiling of 120 wpm**, and delivered
units typically carry far less (one or two lines of 8–15 words
per 10 s unit ≈ 50–90 wpm).

**Is the cap part of the problem? The evidence says yes,
mechanically:**

- At 120 wpm *maximum*, a writer cannot afford the packaging
  that makes speech human. The confession ("She was right.",
  3 words), the doubled emphasis ("Real beautiful.", 2 words),
  the address term (1 word), the reaction fragment ("Usually
  into my face.", 4 words) — each is a small tax. Under a tight
  cap, the rational writer cuts exactly those, because none of
  them carries plot. What survives the cut is the pure move —
  which is precisely the summary register the user indicted.
- The factories' densities show the packaging is not a luxury
  they add after the plot fits; at 201–280 wpm the packaging
  *is* a third to a half of the text. Spongy spends 324 words
  on ten beats — ~32 words per beat, where our units spend
  ~10–20 words per unit on the same class of beat.
- The cap also bans *repetition across lines*: Spongy's word
  "my word" is spent twice (midnight + plea), Cherry's
  "spot/sweet" currency circulates through five speakers, the
  forehead number is said four times in one scene. A ≤20-word
  unit cannot re-spend a phrase; it can barely spend it once.
- What the cap gets right: ESL plainness is NOT the problem.
  justblox12 runs 268 wpm in "aggressively ESL-simple" language
  (rust STUDY §B.5) — short clauses, concrete nouns, almost no
  subordination. Density and plainness coexist in their corpus;
  our cap conflated them, rationing words in the name of a
  simplicity that never required rationing.

**Evidence-only conclusion:** a spoken budget of ~20 words per
10 s sits at roughly half the *dialogue* rate and under half
the *narration* rate of every measured piece in the corpus.
Any fix that keeps the cap and only rewords lines will keep
producing summaries, because the cap is what forces the
summary. (What budget should replace it is the parent's call;
the corpus supports ~30–45 words per 10 s unit for dialogue
scenes and full narration density for narrated pieces, in the
same plain register.)

## 7. RULES FOR THE PARENT'S PEN

Habits, not gates — things to do at the desk, each tied to a
specimen above. They are written to sit *inside* the existing
laws: dialogue-is-action (every line still does a move),
ESL-plain (no slang requirement, no subordination), and the
Mouth Test (read it aloud — these habits are what the aloud
read should be listening for).

1. **Address somebody in the line.** Put the name, kinship word
   or rank word of the person being spoken to inside the
   spoken line — "Sir,", "Dad,", "Yo, Unc," — at least as often
   as not. A line addressed to a person sounds said; a line
   addressed to the audience's notebook sounds written.
   *(Specimen: "Sir, you mind if I box this up and take it home
   for my son?")*
2. **Contract everything a mouth would contract.** Default to
   I'm / don't / gonna-class contractions in dialogue; leave a
   line uncontracted only when the stiffness itself is the
   character (a judge, a form being read aloud). *(Evidence:
   44–60 % contraction rates in every dialogue corpus, §1.)*
3. **Write the ask with the stake inside it.** Never let a
   character request the object alone — the beneficiary, the
   deadline or the fear rides in the same sentence. *(Specimen:
   "take it home **for my son**"; "just give me a few more
   days.")*
4. **Stack the plea; shrink the last ask.** A begging beat is
   3–5 short sentences of escalating offers ending on the
   smallest ask, never one complete sentence of explanation.
   *(Specimen: "Tack on whatever late fee you want. I'll pay
   every dime of that rent. You've got my word. Just don't
   kick us out.")*
5. **Say the important thing twice.** Double the key word or
   phrase inside the beat — admiration, price, accusation —
   instead of intensifying it with an adverb. *(Specimens:
   "Damn, that's beautiful… Real beautiful."; the forehead
   number said four times in one scene.)*
6. **Give each world ONE value-word and let everyone speak
   it.** Dump, fire, refuse and forgive in the same coin —
   fresh/spotty/sweet, my word, the count — so the payoff line
   can return the word with its owner changed. *(Specimen:
   "Sorry, Cherry. I'm too sweet for you now.")*
7. **Let the liar add one helpful detail.** A lie in our
   scripts should be total, casual and overspecified by
   exactly one unrequested detail — and the apology should
   trail one sincerity adverb. *(Specimens: "He is out of the
   country on a business trip. He'll be back soon."; "…with a
   metal spoon, seriously.")*
8. **Confess once per narrator, early.** Any first-person or
   to-camera voice admits one fault against itself in the
   first unit — three to six words, on the record — before it
   is allowed to report anything the audience must believe
   later. *(Specimen: "Mum called me lazy… She was right.")*
9. **Break the sentence where the breath breaks.** At any
   crisis beat, drop to fragments of ≤5 words for at least two
   consecutive lines; let one reaction fragment ("Usually into
   my face.") sit between facts with no plot job at all.
   *(Evidence: 50 % of the whistle narrator's lines are ≤5
   words, clustered at the accident.)*
10. **Quote people; don't paraphrase them.** When the narrator
    reports another character, give that character their own
    short line in their own shape — the shorter the better;
    the ending can ride on a two-word quote. *(Specimens:
    "This one'll reach anything you own."; "My teddy.";
    "Inside the sole. Small letters.")*
11. **Spend one line on self-talk per guilty character.** A
    character alone says his instruction to himself out loud
    ("Be normal. Act like you normally do.") — it characterises
    faster than any description and costs one line.
12. **Budget words like they talk, not like they summarise.**
    Write dialogue units at roughly double the old spoken load
    (~30–45 words per 10 s unit), and spend the increase
    *only* on address, repetition, reaction and confession —
    never on more plot per unit. The plot per unit stays
    exactly as rationed as it is now; it's the packaging that
    was starved. *(Evidence: §6, all five corpora.)*
