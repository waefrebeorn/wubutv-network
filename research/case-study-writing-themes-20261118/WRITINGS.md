## 0. The convergent core — what the field's writing does, across independent studies

These mechanics recur in studies made by different agents, from
different sources, at different times. This is the "based" layer:
evidence, not our gate vocabulary.

1. **One line = one scene = one clip; one speaker per line.**
   8–14 words per line in the soap register (Clippie rule), 8–15 words
   at ~2.5 words/sec in the frutinovela guides; a line runs 4–7
   seconds. [`clippie-ai-20261118/report.md`; `fruit-gen-clones-20261118/report.md`;
   `brainrot-soap-study-20261115/report.md`]
2. **Line 1 is already the conflict.** "Open on the conflict. The
   first line is already a problem"; "the first two seconds must land
   mid-conflict (open on the discovery or the slap, never the setup)."
   [`clippie-ai-20261118/report.md`; `fruit-gen-clones-20261118/report.md`]
3. **A five-beat skeleton:** Hook (conflict stated) → Denial → Evidence
   (shown, not argued) → Turn/Reveal at ~two-thirds → Hanger (final
   line lands and opens the next episode).
   [`fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`; `clippie-ai-20261118/report.md`;
   `brainrot-soap-study-20261115/report.md` Skeleton A/B]
4. **Only four line-types do the work:** (1) accusation with proof,
   (2) lie/denial, (3) entrance line that contradicts the lie,
   (4) authority reveal. "A line that doesn't accuse, conceal, expose,
   or decide does not exist in this genre."
   [`brainrot-soap-study-20261115/report.md`; `fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`]
5. **Lines lean on the line before them ("twining"):** a line is
   twined "when it cannot be moved, reordered, or given to another
   character without breaking — because it borrows its words, its
   grammar, or its facts from the line it answers." Mechanisms:
   echo-completion, question–answer lock, too-specific denial,
   entrance line that re-prices the scene, repetition with escalation.
   [`fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`]
6. **Facts are spent twice, words never:** core fact delivered at
   least twice in different forms (event, then verdict restatement by
   a second voice), plus once per channel (picture + spoken line +
   burned-in caption simultaneously); the only licensed verbatim
   repeat is one key word, escalated.
   [`fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`]
7. **Conflict is carried by a proof object, never reported
   secondhand** — phone with hearts, pregnancy test, suitcase; shown,
   then named in dialogue. [`brainrot-soap-study-20261115/report.md`]
8. **One value change per episode**, stated as a before/after pair;
   "If nothing changed, the episode can be deleted with nothing lost."
   The hanger is a question, an entrance, a one-word answer, or a
   verdict that births the next conflict — and the next episode's
   first beat pays it, at least partly, inside ~5 seconds.
   [`fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`]
9. **Speech rules are written per character before lines** — how they
   ask, lie, refuse (Poptoy specimens in §5 below); the lead carries
   one want that never changes, only the means and allies do.
   [`fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`]
10. **End the line on the hard word** — payload word last, where the
    next line can grab it; silence (gasp/pause) is written in as a
    script beat; names are spent only for emphasis, accusation, or
    entrance in two-character scenes.
    [`fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`]

## 1. Clippie AI — the factory's published method

Source: `clippie-ai-20261118/report.md`. Evidence rests on Clippie's
published pages + third-party reviews; the editor is account-walled,
so workflow is **[OBSERVED]** from documentation, not in-app use.

**Line construction**
- Atomic form: **one line = one scene = one talking clip.** Script
  format `"Name: line"`; extension `"Name (where they are): line"`
  adds a scene note. **[acting tags]** — [whispers], [laughs],
  [gasps], [sighs], [nervous], [long pause] — are "performed, not
  read aloud."
- **8–14 words per line** ("Short lines keep clips at 4–6 seconds and
  pace high"); a scene runs "as long as its line plus a short beat,
  usually four to seven seconds."
- Idea mode: ~30 s / 1 min / 2 min → the writer drafts **6, 11 or 20
  lines**, "with a hook first and a payoff last." Talking-story
  ceiling 40 scenes; recommended early episodes 45–90 s, one twist
  each.
- A usable brief gives **a cast, one conflict, one location and a
  specific ending**; first attempt kept to two characters and a
  single location; between episodes change at least setup, evidence
  and final consequence — "never just swap fruit names in the same
  dialogue."
- Draft discipline: "Read the draft aloud; cut lines that repeat the
  accusation, reveal the joke too soon, or need explanation after
  the ending."

**Cast construction (series bible)**
- Roles: **lead (wants something specific) / rival (blocks, with a
  believable reason) / wildcard (reveals secrets at the worst
  moment) / comic relief**; per character: look, voice, speech
  habits, **one secret**; two or three recurring conflict engines.
- No finer exchange mechanics (answer/top/block at line level) are
  specified in this file beyond conflict/reveal placement.

**Hook / payoff**
- "Open on the conflict. The first line is already a problem" — the
  starter story's first line has a strawberry arriving forty minutes
  late to her own birthday dinner, again.
- **Reveal around two-thirds in**: "The other reservation, the second
  girlfriend, the mask coming off. Everything before it sets it up."
- **End on a line that lands** — "A comeback, a twist, or a cake in
  the face — 'people replay the last line and share it.'"
- Series method: one repeatable situation (shared fridge, market
  stall, apartment kitchen); plan three episodes ahead, each with a
  different joke mechanism; **each episode answers its own opening
  question**. The study notes a tension in Clippie's own material:
  the blog says episodes "usually end on a cliffhanger," the series
  guide "explicitly softens this: connection via cast and situation,
  payoff not withheld." No quote-chaining, no compilation/vault
  tooling.

**Captions / titles**
- Captions auto-generated from synced speech, styled by the user,
  "for viewers watching with the sound off"; QA doctrine: "Check
  captions against the words actually spoken, especially fruit names
  and puns."
- No title grammar is given in this file.

**Verbatim (quoted-in-study)**
- `"Strawberry: I can't believe you did this."` — the "Name: line"
  format example.
- Idea-mode example: `"Apple finds out her best friend Orange has
  been dating her ex"`.
- Homepage demo: `"A strawberry has a blue baby. The banana has
  questions."`

## 2. The clone census — sixteen factories, one recipe

Source: `fruit-gen-clones-20261118/report.md`. Convergence across
factories is marked **[INFERRED]** in the study (§4.4); product facts
are **[OBSERVED]** from vendor pages/docs. No account was created
anywhere.

**Line construction**
- **One line = one scene = one clip** (Common Sauce #2):
  "speaker-attributed single lines; each line becomes exactly one
  scene/clip of ~4–7 seconds… short lines, one speaker, no
  multi-speaker pile-ups inside a unit."
- Frutti clip-blocks: Quick = 2×10 s (~15–20 s), Standard = 3×10 s
  (~30 s), Long = 3×15 s (~45 s), Epic = 4×15 s (~60 s); ~60-s
  ceiling. GhostShorts: episodes 60 or 90 s only (8–12 or 12–18
  scenes), 2–6 speaking characters.
- ToonlyAI DIY route: dialogue written in Veo format — **speaker,
  voice style, colon, words — no quotation marks**, "because quotes
  can leak on-screen text."
- DIY prompt craft (not dialogue): five-layer prompt structure at
  30–50 words per prompt (fruitloveisland.ai guide); Flashloop rule:
  "video prompts describe characters by appearance, never by name" —
  rationale quoted-in-study: "the video model doesn't know who
  'Strawberry' is."

**Dialogue moves**
- Vuela's published 3-step recipe: pick 3–5 fruit characters with
  **one trait each**, write a short dating-show situation with **one
  twist**, **end on a cliffhanger and number it as an episode**;
  Pixar look + dramatic narrator + pop music; regenerate single
  scenes, post as a series.
- Divergent model (Veedio, StoryShort): **one narrator voice reads
  the whole episode including the characters' lines**; per-character
  voices explicitly not the product.
- **No verbatim dialogue lines in `fruit-gen-clones-20261118/report.md`**
  — no character-to-character script excerpts; answer/top/block
  mechanics at line level are not evidenced here beyond the beat map.

**Hook / payoff**
- "The first two seconds must land mid-conflict (open on the
  discovery or the slap, never the setup)… every episode must end on
  a cliffhanger that forces the next-episode follow" (Revid doctrine,
  quoted-in-study).
- Beat map: Line 1 conflict → escalation → reveal at roughly
  two-thirds → final line lands and doubles as the cliffhanger;
  episodes numbered, end open.
- GhostShorts: every episode written as Part 1 ending on a
  cliffhanger; a "Make Part 2" button continues with the same cast.
- Zyvo series mode: **plans 3–10 episodes with the same cast before
  you make the first one** — every episode gets a title, a summary
  and the cliffhanger it ends on, up front (the census's only
  pre-planned season-arc feature). ClipNova: "story memory of the
  last 5 episodes" carries continuity (secondary source).
- Stated gap (the study's §7 finding): **none** of the tools does
  quote-chained serialization, funnel shorts, the FULL STORY vault,
  a worn-stake device, structured cross-episode continuity ledgers,
  poster-frame discipline, or an ear-test (captions are QA'd against
  the script, never against a cold read of the master transcript).

**Captions / titles**
- Captions are part of the render: styled, word-timed, burned in,
  generated from synced speech; sound-off viewing assumed.
- No title grammar analysed in this file.

## 4. Fruit-soap study 2 — story and speech mechanics (the deepest line-level source)

Source: `fruit-soap-study2-20261116/report.md`. Specimens are
**[EVIDENCED]** where the study reproduces actual scripts/transcripts;
practitioner rules are one working creator's codified practice
(Poptoy) unless a second source is named; the study flags its own
generalisations as LEAD-LEVEL where noted.

**Episode anatomy (Microdrama Academy, *Short Drama Foundations*)**
- 90-second episode: **Pickup 0–5 s** (close previous hook) · **Hook
  first 3 s** · **Body 15–70 s — "One event happens, not three"** ·
  **Turn ~10 s** · **Cliffhanger last 3–5 s**.
- Filmustage Beat Engine: **Hook / Friction / Spike / Button**;
  Button: "cut on the question, not the answer — two seconds earlier
  than feels safe."
- Poptoy five-beat sheet for a **20-second** episode: 1. Hook line
  (states the conflict) 2. Denial (lie/refusal) 3. Evidence (photo,
  witness, prop — shown, not argued) 4. Turn (line/reveal that
  changes who holds power) 5. Hanger (last line opens next episode).

**Line-level rules (Poptoy)**
- **One idea per line.**
- **End the line on the hard word** (payload word last, where the
  next line can grab it).
- **Silence is written in as a line** (gasp/pause is a script beat).
- **Names are cut in two-character scenes** — a name is spent only
  for emphasis, accusation, or entrance.

**Twining — the exchange mechanics.** A line is twined "when it
cannot be moved, reordered, or given to another character without
breaking — because it borrows its words, its grammar, or its facts
from the line it answers." Five mechanisms, each observed on a
specimen:
1. **Echo-completion** — the reply borrows the previous line's frame
   and adds the payload ("agreement in form, detonation in
   content"). Negation variant: a verdict opens by negating the
   earlier phrase — "Nobody is going anywhere."
2. **Question–answer lock** — a question "makes the next line
   mandatory"; engineered so any truthful answer is damage; lets the
   answer be "absurdly short while carrying maximum weight."
3. **Too-specific denial** — the denial must be "slightly too
   specific" — "a lie with more detail than the truth would need";
   it answers the accusation with a category, characterizes the
   liar, and loads the evidence beat.
4. **Entrance line that re-prices the scene** — a third party enters
   with a line that quotes/answers/exposes what was just said,
   written "against lines the entering character 'could not have
   heard.'"
5. **Repetition with escalation** — "say the key word again, louder —
   don't rephrase it." [LEAD-LEVEL in study] the quotable beat is
   "almost always a repetition, a one-word answer, or a verdict
   line."

**Voice rules (written before lines)**
- Poptoy speech rules, quoted-in-study: Strawberry "speaks in
  questions when hurt; she never shouts first"; Banana
  "over-explains when lying — he volunteers dates and times nobody
  asked for"; Lemon "deals in one-line sarcasm and always takes the
  last word"; Old Apple "is formal and slow, says 'child' a lot, and
  holds the family secrets."
- Dawnwood micro-drama character card: the lead carries "one want,
  one wound, a rule they will not break, one visible tell, and a
  line nobody else would say"; serial law: "never let the lead's
  goal change; only the means and the allies do."

**Value change and hangers**
- Core law, quoted-in-study: "One episode equals one value change.
  Compare the character's state at the start and at the end. If
  nothing changed, the episode can be deleted with nothing lost, and
  the viewer will notice that before you do." Value-change table:
  Safe → Threatened · Humiliated → Striking back · Suspected →
  Trusted · Trusting → Betrayed · Unaware → Holding a secret ·
  Winning → Losing on a new condition.
- Four hanger forms: (1) spoken question, unanswered; (2) entrance
  mid-scene ("a good cliffhanger is not a scene cut mid-motion, it
  is a door opening: someone walks in, a message arrives, a name is
  spoken"); (3) one-word answer that opens a bigger question;
  (4) **verdict that births the next conflict** — resolving without
  opening is "the same as saying goodbye."
- Payoff rules: **beat one pays the hanger** (the Pickup exists
  solely to close the previous hook fast; checklist: "make sure beat
  one of the next episode answers the previous hanger, at least
  partly"); **pay a little, then open a larger debt** (episodes 2–5
  drop-off cause: "Episode 1's promise went unpaid"); a **running
  brief** is kept per series — "who knows which secret, who is lying
  to whom, and what the last hanger promised."
- Deletion exercise, quoted-in-study: "strike out every line that
  could be deleted without changing the story. If more than three
  lines qualify, rewrite it."
- Negative case: the founding saga's final hanger (a lemon lawyer's
  offer) was **never paid off**; the account vanished mid-arc.
  Deferral survived 8–9 episodes because each episode "paid
  something." Lesson as stated: "A hanger may be rolled over only by
  paying interest on it in the same episode."

**Redundancy placement**
- Core fact delivered **at least twice, in different forms**, in
  exactly four places: (1) **triple-channel statement** (picture +
  spoken line + burned-in caption simultaneously; series tag on
  screen in first second); (2) **verdict restatement** (event → a
  second voice restates it as judgment/record); (3) **end-question**
  (cliffhanger restated as written/spoken question); (4) **recap
  line** (next episode's Pickup / one-line recap). The four-places
  taxonomy is the study's synthesis, LEAD-LEVEL as a genre-wide
  claim.
- Budget formulation: "facts may be spent twice (event + verdict)
  plus once per channel; words may not be spent twice at all" — one
  licensed exception: escalated repetition of a single key word.
- Facts announced **at the moment they bite**, spoken "by the person
  it damages or the authority who enforces it"; [LEAD-LEVEL]
  sequencing pattern: **fact first, feeling second** — no specimen
  opens with an emotion explained later.
- Classic-soap baseline: soap dialogue is "more expositional and 'on
  the nose'" by design for a half-here audience; fruit soap keeps
  the information redundancy and deletes the wording redundancy.

**Verbatim specimens (quoted-in-study, [EVIDENCED])**
- KreatorFlow smoothie scene:
  > **BANANA** *(smooth)*: "I made you a smoothie."
  > **STRAWBERRY** *(suspicious)*: "What flavour?"
  > **BANANA** *(nervous)*: "…Strawberry."
  Study anatomy: "Four beats, three lines, eleven words of
  dialogue" — want carried by the offer; obstacle = her suspicion;
  twist = one word re-pricing every previous line; cliff = her gasp.
  No setup line, no names, no location talk, no feeling stated
  before its cause.
- KreatorFlow confession cam:
  > **STRAWBERRY** *(to camera, annoyed, fast)*: "He said strawberry
  > was his favourite. His FAVOURITE. And then he made a smoothie
  > out of my cousin."
- ToonlyAI "Table for Two":
  > **AVERY**: "Okay. Be cool. It's just dinner with Penny."
  > **PENNY**: "I'm here to review your restaurant."
  Study gloss: false want spoken in line one so the twist has
  something audible to break; the twist is "the same event re-read,"
  delivered as a plain statement of identity.
- Clippie starter script:
  > **STRAWBERRY**: "You told me you were at the farmers' market."
  > **BANANA**: "I was. With Cherry."
- Micro-drama textbook entrance: a CEO enters as a humiliated woman
  is ejected from a lobby and asks, **"Why is my wife standing
  outside?"** — "The old problem resolves, and in the same second a
  new conflict is born, because now the entire office knows."
- Verdict restatement example: "Valentina is your sister."
- Strawberrina meta-line (via Riccio reconstruction, as reported):
  at one birth the doctor is surprised because "finally a wife had
  not cheated."

**Study's own gaps:** no published corpus study measures dialogue

## 5. Brainrot-soap study — the frutinovela format

Source: `brainrot-soap-study-20261115/report.md`. Source-based, not
view-based (TikTok not directly watchable from the study
environment); structures come from two complete production guides
reproducing full episodes with dialogue, plus press and an academic
corpus study.

**Format envelope**
- Short (**24 s – 3 min**), vertical, numbered parts; story "100%
  soap: cheating, secrets, betrayals, reveals, comebacks"; cast
  absurd, feelings played straight.
- Speech rate **≈2.5 words/second**; an 8-s clip holds **≤20 words**;
  working target **8–15 words per line** "so the character has time
  to react."
- **One spoken line per clip, ideally one speaker**; two speakers in
  one clip is the ceiling, each line separately attributed. Built as
  3–4 clips of 6–10 s (Skeleton A).

**Skeletons**
- **Skeleton A, 30-second five-beat:** Hook 0–3 s (image/line raises
  a question instantly) · Setup 3–10 s (who wants what, and where) ·
  Twist 10–17 s (reveal flips the situation) · Clash 17–24 s
  (emotional peak/chaos) · Cliffhanger 24–30 s (open question earns
  the follow). Hard rule, quoted-in-study: "end on an unanswered
  question — 'a punchline gets a laugh, but a question gets a
  follow.'"
- **Skeleton B, four-scene frutinovela** (a real 23.9-s episode,
  "It's your brother", Part 1): (1) accusation with proof,
  (2) denial collapses when a third person walks in, (3) authority
  drops the secret, (4) silent close-up, on-screen text "To be
  continued…"
- Dating-show variant (Fruit Love Island): reality grammar
  substitutes for plot — arrivals, coupling, bombshell newcomer,
  recoupling where someone gets dumped; "The format itself supplies
  the conflict each episode; no lore required."

**Dialogue moves**
- "Dialogue carries ~100% of the story. There is no lore, no
  world-building exposition. Everything the viewer needs is said out
  loud, by a character, in the scene, usually as an accusation, a
  lie, a confession, or a reveal."
- **Only four line-types do all the work**: (1) accusation with
  proof, (2) lie/denial, (3) entrance line that contradicts the lie,
  (4) authority reveal. "A line that doesn't accuse, conceal, expose,
  or decide does not exist in this genre."
- **Characters talk to each other, never to camera** (a defining
  frutinovela rule vs older "talking fruit" videos; §4's confession
  cam is the documented exception form).
- **Want is stated, not implied**: a character is built as name +
  look + voice + **one want** — example quoted-in-study: "to impress
  Penny and save his restaurant" — and the want is legible in their
  first line.
- Voice is a fixed attribute: a **three-word voice spec** repeated
  identically every episode; examples quoted-in-study: "calm, low,
  amused"; "a grave, raspy old grandmother's voice."
- Animal-branch variant: TTS narrator + burned-in captions can carry
  the plot instead of in-character dialogue [LEAD-LEVEL for some
  frutinovelas adding a narrator on top].

**Hook / payoff**
- "A fruit story lives on its first two seconds" (quoted-in-study).
  "The accusation scene IS the opening — no establishing shot, no
  greeting, proof in hand by second one."
- A **proof object carries the conflict** — phone with hearts,
  pregnancy test, suitcase — shown, then named in dialogue;
  "Conflict is never reported secondhand."
- **Cliffhanger is a freeze, not a fade**: silent close-up reaction
  shot + "To be continued…" text, or spoken open question + end-card
  question that matches it ("Who is Penny bringing?").
- **Cut at the peak, not after it** — "Every part 'cuts off at the
  peak of the drama'"; reveal lands, reaction is last frame,
  consequences are next episode.
- Plot capacity, measured: **one accusation chain per ~24–30 s**
  (4 scenes, ~4–6 spoken lines); a 60-s episode holds roughly two

2. **THE GRIEVANCE JAB** — a complaint aimed at a target the

  (930 replies)

## 19. Talking-story creators — the pacing evidence under the dialogue

Source: `talking-story-creators-20261115/report.md` (+ its
`amendments.md`, a proposal document — proposals, not field
evidence).

**Measured/spoken-density evidence [OBSERVED/measured in the study]**
- Converged target in the study: ~80–95 spoken words per 60-s
  episode (50–60% speech fraction), against our then-budget of
  22–25 words per full-talk 10-s unit (88–100% of runtime speaking).
- Evidence table: dialogue share of film fell 60% (1940) to 35.6%
  (2005), now just under 50%; median inter-line gap 1.9 s comedy,
  3.7 s war; Netflix caption cap 20 chars/sec; BBC 160–180 wpm;
  Veo-era guides: 10-s clip ~25 words max, 18–20 comfortable; 8-s
  clip one line of 6–12 words; natural turn gap ~200 ms — so a 2–3-s
  silence reads as meaning. Micro-drama practitioner: ~12–18
  sentences per 90-s episode, ≤12 words per line.
- Practitioner/trade serialization numbers: hook ≤3 s, ONE event /
  one value change per episode, mini-reversal ~20–25 s, cliffhanger
  in the final 3–15 s; 65% of viewers who watch 3 s stay ≥10 s
  (Sprout data).

**Creator mechanics [EVIDENCED in the study]**
- **BigYowie / Bigfoot vlogs:** outline order per scene — major
  actions / emotional shift / which lines need to land — then
  prompt. Personality designed as one verbal sentence (glossed:
  "dry, casually destructive") + one visual anchor repeated in
  every prompt. Self-awareness gag: Bigfoot complains about how the
  world treats him, in plain modern words.
- **Neural Viz / Monoverse:** full TV-format script first — slug
  lines, action, dialogue, blocking — then a storyboard still per
  shot; the creator acts every line himself and maps the
  performance onto the characters. "Smart" = dramatic irony: the
  glurons confidently misread vanished human culture; the viewer
  knows more than the expert.
- **AI Presidents Gaming:** dialogue "written to interrupt —
  overlaps, 'uhs', stutters" — works because the cast dynamic is
  pre-known; a shared visible task with a finish line gives lines a
  referent, pauses a job, and the scene a built-in ending.
- **AI historical POV vlogs:** fixed template, quoted-in-study:
  "POV: You wake up in [place, disaster year]" — title-as-contract
  before line 1, spoken hook inside 3 s, escalation,
  cliffhanger/punchline ending.
- **Dave Clark:** the relatability anchor is a true story statable
  in one sentence. Quoted-in-study: "The technology itself isn't
  emotional. The emotion comes from the context you bring to it." ·
  "Editing is where tension is actually created. How long you stay
  on a face. When you cut. When you don't cut."
- **Waymark, *The Frost*:** one environment for the whole film; the
  first dialogue line is object + action, quoted-in-study gloss:
  ("Pass me the tail", cut to wrong object).
- **Nothing, Forever** [negative evidence]: segment rotation keeps
  dialogue from ever being continuous; its documented failures —
  setups orphaned, conversations never followed up, plans forgotten
  — are the anti-specimen for hanger discipline.
- **Sunspring / Solicitors** [negative control]: a generated script
  that "repeats sentence constructs without any logic"; the actors'
  performance supplied the legibility the text lacked.

**Gaps (study's own §6):** no published study directly reports
% runtime that is speech for film/TV (fractions derived); no
platform publishes a dialogue spec; micro-drama density (180–360
words per 60–120 s, "no dead air") is paywall-retention technology
the study explicitly warns not to copy; "pattern interrupt every X
seconds" has no citable measured basis.
