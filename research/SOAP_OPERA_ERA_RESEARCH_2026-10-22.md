## STRAND A — ONLINE RESEARCH (soap craft × short-form serial × retention)

### A1. The hook must land inside 1.5 seconds — 45 frames, sound-off legible
A CreatorHouse sample of 1,000 Reels (Mar 2026) found median retention drops
**47% in the first 1.5 seconds**, with only another 8% lost between 1.5–3.0s;
the gap between passed and failed Reels is 23 points at 1.5s. The hook frame
is the single most legible image of the part — no title, no fade, no wide
establishing shot. For soap-era parts, this means: **the hook is a picture
first and a sentence never** — a cold arrival must understand the frame in
1.5 seconds with the sound off.
Sources: [1](https://www.jellypod.com/blog/what-is-a-micro-drama) (via ALGO_MAX
corpus); internal ALGO_MAX/03 findings (CreatorHouse/Percee/Socialinsider
numbers saved 2026-10-21).

### A2. Open mid-conflict — "Open on the Blade"
Professional micro-drama craft: every episode opens mid-conflict within three
seconds — a slap half-delivered, an accusation mid-sentence, a contract
slammed on a table. No establishing shots, no throat-clearing, no one walking
into rooms. "If the first line could be cut without losing the conflict, cut
it and open on the second." For the soap era: every 10s part starts at maximum
tension, because the part has no runtime to earn its tension.
Sources: [2](https://skilldb.dev/skills/writing-genres-skills/micro-drama-writing);
[3](https://seastarfilm.com/insights-detail/how-to-write-for-a-vertical-screen);
[4](https://shortdramaalliance.medium.com/how-to-write-a-short-drama-hooks-pacing-and-paywall-basics-171c811fac84).

### A3. One visible change every 1.5 seconds — the beat grid
AutoWeeb's published microdrama grammar (the most concrete second-level pacing
document in the field): **one new cut or movement every 1.5 seconds**, one
minor plot point every 10 seconds, one to two major plot points per minute. A
part with a static stretch longer than 1.5s is bleeding viewers before the
turn arrives. Soap-era implication: the 10s part is not one shot, it is ~6
micro-beats (hook/state/turn/line/cliff land on the grid).
Sources: [5](https://cinevertstudios.com/how-to-write-vertical-drama.html)
(via ALGO_MAX corpus); internal ALGO_MAX/03 findings.

### A4. The shipping unit is 30–45s; the atom is 10s; the episode is 90–100s
Sub-15s Shorts collapsed in reach in 2026 (at 15s you need ~100% retention to
clear the gate); 30–45s is the YouTube Shorts sweet spot, and creator
playbooks put the microdrama episode sweet spot at 90–100s. Short-form
algorithms judge APV (average percentage viewed) on short content — a
30–45s chain of three to four 10s parts clears gates a single 10s atom never
can. Soap-era structure: **parts chain into pieces (30–45s), pieces assemble
into episodes (90–100s)**. Nothing ships as a lone 10s atom.
Sources: [6](https://salivity.github.io/video-production/article/how-algorithms-reward-video-length-and-engagement);
internal ALGO_MAX/03 findings (Socialync/Socialinsider/Klap numbers);
[7](https://theinnerdetail.com/why-2-min-microdrama-is-the-fastest-booming-niche-for-content-creators/).

### A5. One turn per part — the cliffhanger ladder, not a three-act outline
"Each episode contains exactly one reversal or revelation and ends on the
turn or its immediate consequence. An episode with no turn is filler; an
episode with two turns is wasting one." Outline the serial as a numbered list
of turns before writing anything. For the soap era: each 10s part = one turn,
no more — the turn is the unit of currency, and spending two in one part
starves the next.
Sources: [2](https://skilldb.dev/skills/writing-genres-skills/micro-drama-writing);
[5](https://cinevertstudios.com/how-to-write-vertical-drama.html).

### A6. One withheld fact per part, spent by the next
The microdrama grammar: each episode is one scene with one turn, and it cuts
on a question. The CEO9/CEO10 grammar already says this (cliff frame holds
the withheld fact) — the field confirms it is the load-bearing mechanic, not
a style choice. For the soap era: **the cliffhanger is inventory** — every
part ends holding exactly one unspent fact, and the next part's hook spends
it. A part that resolves everything is a dead end in the chain.
Sources: [7](https://www.jellypod.com/blog/what-is-a-micro-drama);
[5](https://cinevertstudios.com/how-to-write-vertical-drama.html).

### A7. The cliff frame IS the next part's first frame
Frame-chaining is both a continuity technique and an algorithm technique: the
viewer's eye is already in position when the next part begins, and the model's
eye is too. The cliffhanger (narrative) and the first frame (technical) are
the same object. This is the mechanic the owner ordered ("last frame capture
to first frame gen") and the field validates it from both sides.
Sources: [8](https://www.wildpines.ai/blog/how-i-actually-make-ai-video/)
("last-frame chain: export last frame, use as starting image; the seam
disappears"); [9](https://pixeldojo.ai/guides/pixverse-v6-prompting-guide)
(Pixverse V6 first-frame chaining); internal ALGO_MAX/03 findings.

### A8. Weave A/B/C storylines — one storyline owns each part
Daytime serial craft: the A (main), B (supporting/serialized), and C (light
texture) storylines each advance every act, climaxes sequenced C→B→A so each
resolution raises the stakes for the next. Act breaks shift focus between
storylines, giving each a fresh start. Soap-era adaptation: a 90–100s episode
(= 9–10 parts) can run an A-story across 5 parts, a B-story across 3, and a
C-story runner across 2 — **each part belongs to exactly one storyline**,
which is how a 10s atom carries serial weight without lore overload.
Sources: [10](https://fiveable.me/tv-writing/unit-2/a-b-storylines/study-guide/waDPbIa19i9duUlP);
[11](https://www.chipswritinglessons.com/2020/08/05/bookbag-deconstructing-the-writing-process-behind-the-sopranos/)
(Chase's A/B/C/D strands, beats on a wipe-off board);
[12](https://www.euroscript.co.uk/blog/television-writing-getting-into-shape-parttwo)
(soap storylining: A/B/C + story document continuity);
[13](https://charles-harris.co.uk/2014/06/story-structure-legs-1/)
(the serial element links each episodic bead to the next).

### A9. Every part must stand alone for cold arrivals
Micro-drama viewers arrive by swiping mid-series; short-drama apps show the
first 8–10 episodes free and retention hinges on the first three episodes and
the first paywall. The ALGO_MAX rule holds: **no part may require the previous
part to be understood**. The soap era's serialized threads must pay off for
returning viewers without taxing first-timers — each part's hook is
self-sufficient; the thread is a bonus, not a prerequisite.
Sources: [7](https://www.jellypod.com/blog/what-is-a-micro-drama);
[14](https://geekvibesnation.com/cost-to-finish-short-drama/) (8,000+ dramas:
~55 episodes, ~9 free, cliffhanger→paywall);
[15](https://www.asiatvforum.com/en-gb/blog/industry-insights/beyond-views-the-critical-microdrama-metrics-the-industry-still-isnt-measuring.html)
(the three critical moments: first 3s, first 3 episodes, first paywall).

### A10. One spoken line per part, ≤9 words, at 7–9s — the line reframes the turn
Micro-drama dialogue doctrine: one spoken line per shot, under 15 words,
rewritten to sound spoken not written — contractions, one broken-off sentence,
one thing left unsaid. The CEO grammar tightens it to ≤9 words inside 7–9s,
spoken by a named character. The line's job is not exposition; it **reframes
the turn** that just happened. In the soap era, speaking is the new
information channel the bumper era deliberately left empty — one line, placed
late, lands harder than any monologue.
Sources: [16](https://catalora.ai/articles/how-to-make-ai-short-drama)
(HOOK/PRESSURE/TWIST; one spoken line per shot; BEFORE/AFTER spoken rewrite);
[17](https://writersimranthakur.com/2026/02/27/how-to-write-a-micro-drama-script-that-meets-industry-standards/)
(dialogues short & punchy; show don't tell; 1–3 characters);
internal CEO9/CEO10 orders (≤9-word rule).

### A11. Every line escalates or reveals — "write opera, not Chekhov"
"Every line either escalates the conflict or reveals information; lines that
merely characterize get cut. Subtext is a luxury — characters say the
confrontational thing, in front of witnesses, at maximum stakes." Zinger
craft: a fraction of the words intensifies the scene. Soap-era rule: with
only one line per 10s, **no line is allowed to be texture**. It either turns
the plot or it does not ship.
Sources: [2](https://skilldb.dev/skills/writing-genres-skills/micro-drama-writing);
[18](https://thewritepractice.com/effective-dialogue/);
[19](https://onemoredraft.com/downloads/ebook-omd-how-to-write-dialogue.pdf)
(white space is speed; silence is a line; the unanswered question).

### A12. Recurring locked cast is the retention engine
"Characters are the chains that bind your story together. You can spend every
episode in a different place... as long as your characters are there for the
ride." Sitcoms drive 13.4% of Hulu's subscriber retention from 7.6% of the
catalog on longevity + rewatchability; comfort rewatching runs on
predictable structure and familiar faces. For the soap era: the locked cast
(UNC, LAURA, COLONEL, PETER + the R-series) is not decoration — it is the
reason a viewer survives the 1.5s cliff of part 40. **Recognition without
interruption** is the continuity standard: the viewer must never stop and
wonder whether the person changed.
Sources: [20](http://www.nightmaresofawriter.com/2012/08/the-familiarity-conundrum-and-episodic.html);
[21](https://www.parrotanalytics.com/insights/sitcoms-are-the-unsung-heroes-of-streaming-subscriber-loyalty/);
[22](https://bleedingfool.com/movies-tv-film/the-rise-of-comfort-rewatching-why-fans-return-to-the-same-shows-again-and-again/);
[23](https://design-rise.com/consistent-characters-ai-video/) ("recognition
without interruption").

### A13. Fixed locations are the apartment sets — the world holds still so the story can move
Sitcom retention runs on loose storylines + short episodes + familiar places;
short-drama audiences return multiple times a day (Adjust: sessions per user
rose 1.61→2.05 by day 30) because the format is a habit, not an event. The
WuBuTV answer: the MEGA corridor, the storage units, the vending machine,
the station desk — **a small set of fixed locations the audience learns like
a sitcom apartment**. In an AI-serial, fixed locations are also a continuity
technology: the fewer new rooms to invent, the fewer rooms to drift.
Sources: [21](https://www.parrotanalytics.com/insights/sitcoms-are-the-unsung-heroes-of-streaming-subscriber-loyalty/);
[24](https://www.marketing-interactive.com/apac-cant-stop-scrolling-as-short-drama-apps-surge);

### A14. The comfort loop is the format — "the network continues"
Micro-drama's deepest mechanic is not the twist but the return: average
sessions run ~20 episodes once a series hooks a viewer; retained users form
multiple daily touchpoints. The WuBuTV canon already encodes this as premise:
the standing order reads "continue," everyone was already continuing. Soap-era
directive: **the serial's engine is continuity itself** — the same people, the
same rooms, one new turn per part, forever. The loop is the product.
Sources: [26](https://www.thegutenberg.com/blog/microdrama-brand-storytelling/);

### B1. The wordmark gate survives — `~ WuBu ~` on every UNC frame

### B2. Cast lock becomes cast-hue lock — one character owns one hue
CEO7's six-color palette (COBALT/VERMILION/CADMIUM/BONE/INK/FOREST; two

PETER = VERMILION. In a chained serial this is a **drift firewall**: even if
a face wobbles between parts, the hue ownership keeps recognition instant —
the viewer reads "VERMILION blazer" before they read the face. Every part's
prompt re-states the hue lock; it is as load-bearing as the name.
Source: canon (CEO7 PRIMARY_COLOR_DESIGN; STATION_PIECE_01_3PARTS template).

### B3. Lore objects become serial settings, not plot devices
THE MEGA, THE STORAGE UNITS, THE VENDING MACHINE — the wizard logic treats
them as places where work happens, never as mysteries to solve. The
UNIVERSE_EXPANSION arcs prove the mechanic: A-001 THE KEY RING (6 units, the
ring grows one key per unit), A-003 THE RESTOCK LOOP (the same ten minutes
from both sides of the glass), A-004 THE PENNY LEDGER (one denomination per
unit). Soap-era directive: **arcs live inside one location for 4–6 parts** —
the location is the continuity anchor the chained frames need.
Source: canon (UNIVERSE_EXPANSION_v1.md Parts III–IV; premises 79–84).

### B4. Commentary = the editing/assembly layer — assembly IS the commentary now
The wizard hard rule: commentary is the editing/assembly layer, never TTS over
native speech; AI clip audio with speech-like artifacts ships silent. The soap
era inverts the old silence (now there IS a spoken line per part) but keeps
the rule: **the line is native to the generation, and the commentary voice is
the cut itself** — part order, pacing, where the line lands, what the cliff
frame withholds. The editor is the author; the assembly is the voice.
Source: user's WuBuMovie notes (editing-layer philosophy; no-dub rule);
UNIVERSE_EXPANSION_v1.md (recursive loop: factories read canon, never write).

### B5. Callback budget: one per part, first-timer still gets the joke
The continuity web's standing rules: max one callback per unit; a callback
rewards the returning viewer but the first-timer still gets the joke; new bits
get an ID before they ship; arcs get ledger IDs (`arc:A-002/5`). The soap era
is callback-dense by nature (serial memory is the point), so the budget is
the guardrail that keeps parts cold-arrival-safe: **one callback per part,
max; the part's own beat must land without it**.
Source: canon (CONTINUITY_WEB; UNIVERSE_EXPANSION_v1.md serialization
mechanics; premise 80: "a callback is a form of paperwork").

### B6. Character engines: who drives which storyline
The canon's pairing rules map cleanly onto the A/B/C weave: **LAURA is the
only reactor** — she is the audience surrogate, so she owns the A-story's
emotional turns; **THE COLONEL catastrophises** (irony in the words) — the
A-plot escalation engine; **PETER's enthusiasm is not evidence** — the B-plot
complication engine (he promises what the station cannot keep); **UNC** (may
react under STATION grammar) anchors the C-runner deadpan; **DRIFT-PROOF
roles** (Dispatcher voice, Caller 6, Chicken Keeper hands) carry recurring
bits at zero continuity cost across all three storylines. Two characters can
share a frame but never a plot — the plot belongs to the world.
Source: canon (UNIVERSE_EXPANSION_v1.md pairing rules; premises 81, 83);
STATION template cast list.

### B7. The cliffhanger graduates: administrative → soap
The canon's deadpan-safe cliffhanger was administrative: a form half-filled,
a key unreturned, a count unreconciled. The soap era keeps the SHAPE (a
withheld fact, filed not explained) and upgrades the STAKES: the withheld
fact is now a story fact — who walked in, what the line meant, what the
count was. Same discipline (withhold exactly one, spend it next part), new
fuel (people want things from each other, not just from paperwork). The
administrative cliffhanger was the training weight; the soap cliffhanger is
the lift.
Source: canon (UNIVERSE_EXPANSION_v1.md serialization mechanics; premise 82);
CEO9 (retired "melodrama at full gravity over an object that doesn't deserve
it" — the soap era's melodrama is aimed at people, which is where it belongs).

## STRAND C — FIRST-FRAME CHAINING PIPELINE (docs only, nothing generated)

Tool docs: `media.generate_video` accepts an ordered `prompt` list of
`{kind, value}` entries — `kind: text` carries the verbatim request text,
`kind: image` carries a **local still-image path**. The tool does not rewrite
the prompt; the subagent expands it. `resume_from_snapshot_id` continues the
same generation context across turns without resending the image.

### The pipeline, step by step

1. **Render part N** with the full prompt (identity lock + beat labels +
   camera move + spoken line + negatives), start image = part N−1's approved
   cliff frame (for part 1, the approved keyframe/composed still).
2. **Capture the cliff frame**: `ffmpeg -sseof -0.2 -i partN.mp4 -frames:v 1
   partN_cliff.jpg` (last ~0.2s; matches the 9.0–10.0s CLIFF window).
3. **Approve the cliff frame before chaining.** Check: identity lock intact
   (wordmark, hue, face), no text drift, composition has room for the next
   move. If it drifted, do NOT chain from it — fall back to the canonical
   reference frame and re-stage. (Rule from vendor practice: "approve the
   extracted frame before passing it forward; return to the original character
   reference if it already contains an identity error.")
4. **Generate part N+1**: `media.generate_video` with `prompt = [{kind:
   image, value: partN_cliff.jpg}, {kind: text, value: partN+1_prompt}]`.
   The text describes **only what moves and what the camera does** — never
   re-describe what is already visible in the frame; the identity lock travels
   as a fixed verbatim block appended to every part's text.
5. **Remove the shared frame at the edit.** The chained frame exists at the
   end of part N and the start of part N+1; when concatenating, drop the
   duplicate (documented Runway operation: "Use → Use current frame", then
   adjust timing and remove the shared frame at the join) or the seam stutters.
6. **Cap the chain.** Re-anchor to canonical references every ~4 parts: the
   chained outputs are increasingly imperfect copies — vendor rule: "use the
   same approved references instead of chaining from increasingly imperfect
   outputs." A 9–10 part episode = two to three anchored chains, not one
   10-link chain.
7. **Change as few variables as possible per generation.** One new thing per
   part (the turn); everything else — light, lens, palette, wardrobe, framing
   language — stays verbatim. Bundling three changes into one part is how the
   logo quietly redraws itself.
8. **Budget the cap.** `media.generate_video` allows at most 6 successful
   videos per user message (delegated agents count too); a 3–4 part piece =
   3–4 videos, so a shipping piece fits in one message and a 90–100s episode
   (9–10 parts) spans two messages by design.

### Gotchas (from vendor docs)

- **Image-to-video seeds only the first frame** — identity can still drift
  *within* the clip. First-frame chaining fixes the JOINT, not the clip
  interior; the interior is guarded by the verbatim identity lock + one-move-
  per-beat prompts + shot-aware reference packs (front/three-quarter/full-body).
- **First-frame + last-frame (native)** is more reliable than chaining when
  both ends are known: Seedance 2.0/2.5, Kling 3.0, Hailuo 02, Wan 2.6, Luma
  and Pixverse V6 all accept an end frame; the model interpolates between two
  locked states. Use native first+last for single coherent moves; use chaining
  for open-ended serial continuation.
- **Match still aspect ratio and resolution to the target output** (at least
  1080×1920 for 1080p vertical; 1280×720 stills for our 16:9 lane) — mismatch
  forces crop/hallucination at the joint.
- **First-frame rules**: subject centered and clear, no clutter/low contrast,
  same framing across the chain; prompt carries motion + camera + lighting/mood
  so the look holds.
- **The start image must have somewhere to go**: a tight close-up leaves no
  push-in; no motion blur or lens flare in the still (they read as movement and
  fight the real motion).
- **Every word in the frame is a word the model must hold steady** — zero
  on-screen text except diegetic digits/labels keeps the joint clean.
- Tool mechanics: `resume_from_snapshot_id` continues the same video context
  across turns instead of resending the image; set `output_dir` for working
  assets (takes/, not the Media tab); `name` stems keep the part files
  addressable; the tool copies the request verbatim — sloppy prompt text is
  sloppy video.

## HOW THE WIZARD LOGIC ENTERS THE SOAP ERA — production directives

1. **Ship in 3–4 part chains (30–45s); assemble 90–100s episodes from pieces.**
   Ten seconds is the atom, never the shipment. A 9–10 part episode spans two
   video-budget messages by design (≤6 videos per message).
2. **One spoken line per part: ≤9 words, 7–9s, named character, reframes the
   turn.** The line is native to the generation. Silence counts as a line when
   native audio carries the beat (one hard sound). No line is texture — it
   turns the plot or it does not ship.
3. **Cliff frame = next part's first frame.** Capture with
   `ffmpeg -sseof -0.2`, approve before chaining, feed as the start image.
   Remove the shared frame at the edit. Re-anchor to canonical references
   every ~4 parts — chains longer than that drift.
4. **Cast-hue locks hold across the whole serial; `~ WuBu ~` on every UNC
   frame.** Two primaries max per frame + bone + ink. Hue is the drift
   firewall: the viewer reads the VERMLION blazer before the face.
5. **One storyline owns each part (A/B/C weave); act breaks shift the
   storyline.** Every part stands alone for cold arrivals; max one callback
   per part, and the part's beat must land without it.
6. **Fixed locations are the apartment sets** (MEGA corridor, storage units,
   vending machine, station desk). Arcs live inside one location for 4–6
   parts. Drift-proof voice/hands roles (Dispatcher, Caller 6, Chicken
   Keeper) carry the recurring bits at zero continuity cost.
7. **Editing is the commentary.** Part order, pacing, line placement, and what
   the cliff frame withholds are the authorial voice. Native audio untouched;
   TTS dub never. The assembly is the show's voice, the way the wizard's
   commentary was always the edit.
8. **The cliffhanger graduates from administrative to soap.** Withhold exactly
   one fact per part and spend it in the next — same discipline as the
   form-half-filled, new fuel: people want things from each other now, not
   just from paperwork.
