### ONE MILLION PETALS (the arena/prize show — this batch is its reference cut)

1. **THE REFEREE PRINTS THE RULES.** Open every episode with
   the host on a balcony/above the arena stating the rules in
   one breath and printing them on the set: who fights, in what
   order, for how many petals. The creativestorydiy father
   ("Red, blue, golden: one by one") is the shape — a cold
   viewer should be able to repeat the rules after unit 1.
2. **THE LADDER IS THE EPISODE.** One opponent per unit, each
   visibly stronger (frost → fire → strong), each verdict a
   body on the ground. The petals prize is counted the way
   this factory counts everything: printed, on a banner, in
   the world — ONE MILLION over the arena gate, re-shown in
   every unit.
3. **RULE-BREAKING IS THE ESCALATION, AND IT GETS NAMED.**
   When the villains break the printed rule (all attack at
   once), the referee names the foul from above in the same
   unit — "I said one on one!" — so cheating reads as a beat,
   not as confusion. The hero's answer is one line ("Go ahead
   and try") plus one picture (three bodies down).
4. **TRAINING IS A BODY LEDGER.** Between bouts, show the
   challenger being remade in single-image units — ice room,
   heat room, weights that won't lift — each answered by the
   father's one-line refusal ("Not yet") or the brothers'
   mockery. Preparation must change the contestant's surface
   (charred, cracked, glowing) so the arena crowd — and the
   viewer — can see the training when he walks in.
5. **THE PRIZE IS NEVER PAID CLEANLY.** Both features end with
   the authority grabbing at the revealed fortune ("A fortune!
   Burned, for him?") or stalling the payout. Let the petal
   count be grabbed at, re-tested, and re-fought at the button
   — the arena stays open because the prize is always one
   strike away from changing owners.
6. **ONE TEST, RE-SPENT.** "One strike, and we'll know what
   you're worth" carries 411 seconds because every act is the
   same test in a new costume. Petals should pick ONE physical
   test (a strike, a weigh-in, a petal-count poured into a
   jar) and re-run it in every unit with higher stakes — never
   a new mechanic per unit.

## TRANSPLANT — for SHE TOOK MY FACE

1. **THE DEFECT ASKED IN SECOND ONE.** Open on the question, not the
   event: "Why is your face not moving?" / "That is not your face. Why
   are you wearing it?" The taken face should have one visible tell —
   it doesn't blink, it smiles a beat late, it never cries — and the
   first line points straight at it.
2. **THE FASHION LIE.** The thief's cover for the tell is a trend claim:
   "It's the new look. Everyone wears a still face now." A lie the
   audience can see through in the same frame it is spoken — dramatic
   irony with zero exposition.
3. **THE LABEL WITH THE WRONG FACE (from the shampoo episode).** The
   stolen identity gets packaged: her face printed on the invader's
   products, her name under the invader's picture. Show the tube/poster/
   bottle — the theft as merchandise, with the thief's branding where
   her face belongs.
4. **THE SUBSTANCE LEDGER.** Give the theft a fill level: each episode
   the invader has taken measurably more (her face on more doors, her
   voice in more mouths, her name on more lists) and the real one's
   face is measurably fainter. A cold viewer reads the score off the
   bodies in any frame.
5. **THE CONFESSION LINE-SHAPE.** The victim's want, said once, plainly,
   to the person whose place she wants back: "I just wanted my face.
   Like yours. Mine." Shape: "I just wanted to be ___, like you." Never
   a speech — one line, answered by the law ("In this house, faces stay
   where they are put. Always.") and by a defender ("Stop. She has lost
   enough tonight.").
6. **THE ENFORCER'S TELL (the curtain).** Whoever polices the theft —
   the mother, the landlady, the face-keeper — carries the same tell.
   End the episode on the victim's quiet question ("Why does YOUR face
   do that?") and a final frame of the enforcer's face slipping. The
   accuser's body betrays her; that picture is the next episode's hook.
7. **THE CALM CAFÉ PAYOFF.** When the victim finally acts, don't show
   the act — show the users in chaos (faces melting, names failing) and
   cut to her, alone, calm, drinking tea. Revenge legible as contrast:
   their disorder, her stillness.

## TRANSPLANT — for MY LANDLORD IS A BABY (our storybook-track show)

1. **Build every episode on ONE want that can be drawn.** Not "the tenant is sad" — a tenant standing outside a door, a crib, a bath, a fridge that the baby-landlord controls. If the want can't be drawn as one arrangement of bodies and objects, it isn't the episode's want yet.
2. **Print the stake in the world in the first seconds.** The baby landlord's power should be a visible object arrangement: the only key worn as the baby's rattle, the rent book used as the baby's pillow, the tenant's shoes lined up outside the baby's door. The suitcase-of-cash rule: motive sits in the open, next to the culprit, before anything happens.
3. **Run the fairy-tale chain and nothing else.** Refused → dreams → a friend acts → cost paid act by act → want granted. No B-plot, no lore cutaway, no parallel thread. Chronology is the structure; the premise (a baby is the landlord) is re-spent by the chain itself, since every refusal and every payment passes through the baby's hands.
4. **Show the cost act by act.** If the tenant earns the rent, we watch the work in completed small actions — carried, stacked, handed over — the way post 2 shows sacks, cash, shop, carry, plug. Never elide a link the plot depends on; the labour *is* the middle of the episode.
5. **Spend text once, at the midpoint, on the want.** One plain sentence — a note on the fridge, a line on the rent book, one spoken sentence — that names the want after the pictures have taught it ("I want to sleep in my own bed."). Simplest possible English. Everything before it teaches it; everything after it pays it.
6. **End by re-shooting the opening picture with the injustice corrected.** Episode opens: tenant on the doorstep, baby inside with the key. Episode ends: same doorstep composition, tenant inside, baby asleep on the tenant's shoulder. The two frames, flipped between, must tell the whole episode.
7. **Cast by silhouette and keep the sorting permanent.** The baby is always the smallest body with the biggest objects (huge key, huge rent book, throne-like high chair); the tenant is always the one carrying things. A viewer joining any episode cold sorts everyone in one frame and never revises it.

3. **Answer every silent beat with its physical opposite or consequence in the very next shot.** Hot → water. Taken → empty hands shown. Seated → the chair gives way. No beat may be answered by a look alone; a look is allowed only at poster size (full-frame, held) as the payoff picture.

## TRANSPLANT — for SHE TOOK MY FACE

1. **THE RECOGNITION REFUSAL scene.** Open an episode at the door: the
   taken-over home character comes home and her own family asks "Who are
   you?" She says "I am your wife / your mother." Answer: "My wife had
   HER face. You are a stranger." Three lines, no exposition — the theft
   is proven by the refusal, on a doorstep, in frame 1.
2. **THE RESTORATION ORDER.** Let the victim try to buy herself back.
   She lists the stolen things as a spoken shopping list: "Give me my
   face back. And my voice. And my name on the door." Itemized identity
   makes the theft countable — the audience can check items off as the
   serial takes or returns each one.
3. **THE FIXER WHOSE BODY IS THE TELL.** The one who offers to restore
   her is already wearing a taken face that doesn't fit (wrong face on
   the wrong body, seams showing). His pitch line-shape: "I will make
   you exactly like me." The audience reads the con off his body while
   she can't.
4. **THE BOTCHED RESTORATION.** One fix attempt must go visibly wrong —
   she gets A face back, but the wrong one (the invader's face, a
   neighbour's face). The payoff is a mirror shot: she looks, and the
   wrong face looks back. Horror carried by one picture, zero lines.
5. **THE REPLACEMENT IN THE BED.** Show the theft's prize, not the theft:
   the invader in her house, in her chair, being served her dinner,
   while the real one watches from the window in her diminished body.
   One frame states who won.
6. **THE VOW FROM THE GUTTER.** End episodes on her vow spoken inside
   the stolen space ("This is my house. I will take my face back."),
   then let the invader's fist / door / laugh answer it. The promise is
   the serial engine; the answer keeps it honest.
7. **BRAID TWO THEFTS.** Run the face-theft arc against a second,
   smaller body-theft (a voice taken, a name taken) so quiet stretches
   in one arc ride the other's dialogue. Each arc re-spends the premise:
   identity is the body, and the body can be stolen piece by piece.

Honest verdict: original serial installment, not a repost — the account's
own braid, and the recognition-refusal + restoration-scam machinery is
new craft beyond study #2, not a repeat.
