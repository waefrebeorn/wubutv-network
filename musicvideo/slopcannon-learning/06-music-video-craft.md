# 06 — Music-video craft

Sources: https://slop.cc/wiki/music-video and
https://slop.cc/wiki/performance-video (both updated 2026-09-30).

## Music video guide [confirmed]

- "A music video is a moving-image work organized around a song or musical
  recording. It can tell a story, stage a performance, explore an image, or
  switch between all three. The useful question is what the pictures make the
  music feel like."
- Visual proposition: "A strong proposition is something you can picture
  happening: a lonely supermarket becomes an ocean, a singer keeps encountering
  earlier versions of herself, or every object in a bedroom gradually floats
  upward. This gives individual shots a shared reason to exist. A list of
  handsome locations provides options, but it does not yet explain why the
  viewer should travel between them."
- Performance and concept overlap; "a narrative passage can disappear when the
  chorus needs direct address."
- Map change to musical sections: "Listen once for the song's architecture
  before making a shot list. Mark the introduction, changes of vocal energy,
  instrumental breaks, and the final release. Then assign each section a
  visual state." Example: first verse boxes the performer into narrow
  doorways; the chorus finally reveals the room around them.
- "Not every drum hit requires a cut. Try holding through several accents so
  a later edit feels consequential. A movement can begin before an accent and
  arrive on it, or a cut can intentionally oppose the rhythm. Make those
  decisions on the actual soundtrack; a prompt asking for perfect beat
  synchronization does not establish an edited timeline."
- Recurring vocabulary: "Choose two or three recurring elements and let their
  meaning change. A red chair can be empty, occupied, overturned, and finally
  carried outside." Keep costume, prop, environment description stable;
  change the action or framing. Keep an establishing view available so
  expressive close-ups still belong to a place.
- Rough cut first: "Lay out rough images or short tests against the whole
  song early. This reveals whether the concept develops, peaks too soon, or
  runs out of ideas after the first minute. Reserve one visual event for the
  ending."
- "Judge transitions by what carries across the cut: gesture, screen
  direction, color, shape, or emotional tension."
- Common mistakes: illustrating every lyric literally until the video has no
  independent visual idea; spending the strongest reveal immediately and
  repeating it unchanged; assuming generated movement will land on musical
  accents without checking the edit.

## Performance video guide [confirmed]

- "A performance video makes the act of performing its main event: a singer
  addresses the lens, a band inhabits a room, or a dancer gives the music a
  body. Its drama comes from presence and changing energy, even when there is
  no separate plot."
- Staged vs live: a staged performance "may instead be played to a finished
  recording, with multiple takes assembled afterward" — which is what a
  Slopcannon cut is. Establish which one you are making; the relationship
  between gesture, sound, and editorial freedom changes.
- Eyeline: "Looking into the lens can feel confrontational or intimate;
  looking beyond it can suggest a real audience or private concentration."
- Coverage as emotional progression: "Begin with three useful views: a wide
  shot that explains the space, a medium shot that reads posture and hands,
  and a close-up that makes a particular emotional change visible. Additional
  angles should contribute something those views cannot. Five nearly
  identical waist-up shots offer less editorial freedom than three shots with
  different dramatic jobs."
- Body connected to music: "Watch points of contact: fingers on strings, a
  mallet meeting a drumhead, breath before a sung phrase, weight transferring
  before a dancer lands." And the line that justifies our G4 gate: "If the
  source recording has a sustained vocal note, a visibly idle mouth can break
  the illusion even in otherwise attractive footage."
- "In generated scenes, complicated instrument technique and precise vocal
  synchronization deserve separate tests. Establish a strong pose and readable
  gesture before asking for elaborate playing."
- Restraint: "Try a still camera during a quiet phrase and save movement for
  the performer's expansion into the chorus. Alternatively, keep the staging
  constant and let the face do the work. Presence is not measured by how many
  effects surround it."
- "Build the edit around complete expressive units: a glance followed by a
  response, a preparatory breath followed by a phrase, or a gesture followed
  by its release. Cutting away before every action finishes can make even a
  compelling artist feel strangely absent. Review the sequence with sound,
  then silently; the body should still suggest an intentional rhythm."
- Common mistakes: calling a playback performance a live recording; cutting to
  instrument close-ups that contradict the sound; so much camera movement the
  performer never becomes the center of attention.

## WuBu mapping

The music-video guide is the backing for our banger test and chorus-hook test:
one visual proposition, sections mapped to visual states, two or three
recurring elements whose meaning changes, one reserved ending event. The
performance guide backs SINGING/CUTAWAY discipline and the rule that a vocal
scene must show a vocal — an idle mouth on a sustained note is a fail even in
a beautiful take. The "prompt asking for perfect beat synchronization does not
establish an edited timeline" line is the wiki's own support for our honest
limit: we cut on lyric times and judge plausibility; we do not claim
beat-perfect sync.
