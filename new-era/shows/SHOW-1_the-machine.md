# SHOW 1 — THE MACHINE

**Status:** founding treatment, new era. STATION is closed canon (585 pieces,
ep079, ST-079; piece 585 'The Essay the Millpond Keeps for No Reader' landed after shutter as archive only). THE MACHINE
inherits STATION's cast, rooms, and lore — it does not continue STATION's
plots.

## Premise

Inside the failing station, a small crew makes music videos for a singer who
is not a person. The machine writes the song, times every word, fires the
takes, and cuts the master — and the crew's job is to keep the mouths on the
words. It works, most days. But the machine has started mishearing its own
song: a hook nobody wrote, a word the transcription drops every time, a take
that sings the right line at the wrong speed. The crew fixes it, the machine
thanks them, and the fix does not hold. Something is wrong, and the wrongness
is learning the crew's names. (From the user's 2026-11-03 order and the
2026-11-05 draft: about making music videos, making content, being inside
the AI creation machine, with a slow-building self-awareness that something
is wrong.)

## The 10-second transition device — THE MOUTH HANDOFF

Every 10-second unit ends on a sung word's onset — a mouth visibly opening on
a word. The next unit opens on the SAME mouth, mid-word, from a different
angle or a different singer. The cut is a lip-sync handoff: the word carries
the audience across the cull. If the mouths do not match, the transition
fails on screen and the crew notices — the show's central craft problem IS
its transition device. Rule: no unit ends on a closed mouth; no unit opens
on a freeze. The song is the tempo gospel — the beat grid is the contract
between units, and a cut that lands off the grid is a visible mistake the
characters have to live with.

## The world rule (what the characters know about the cull)

The crew knows the world rebuilds itself every ten seconds — they have the
call sheets to prove it. What they do NOT know is whether the machine rebuilds
the world, or the world rebuilds the machine. They work in ten-second shifts
because the machine only guarantees ten seconds. A take that runs long is a
take the machine has already forgotten. The slow build: the machine's fixes
keep arriving BEFORE the mistakes they fix.

## Episode shape

- One episode = 3–4 units of 10s (30–40s), one conversation across the whole
  episode, exactly one spoken line per unit at 7–9s, ≤9 words, named
  character. (STATION grammar, kept.)
- Unit structure: 0–1.5 HOOK (the wrongness shows), 1.5–4 STATE (the crew
  works), 4–7 TURN (the machine answers wrong), 7–9 LINE, 9–10 CLIFF (a
  mouth opens on a word that is not in the song — the next unit's first
  frame).
- Units chain by the mouth handoff: the cliff mouth IS the next unit's hook
  mouth. A chain's final cliff is the next chain's first frame (bridge
  cliff, STATION arc rule, kept).
- The show is cut like an MV: every word on screen synced to the word in the
  song; word-sync, tempo gospel, one 16:9 master, CRF 18, AAC.

## Pilot episode chains (dialogue-first — one conversation each)

### Chain M1-1 — "The Dropped Word" (OPEN)
Stake: LAURA's credibility with UNC — if she is wrong, she is the new hire
crying wolf; if she is right, the machine is lying.
Setting: the cutting room, after the dropped-word night.

- LAURA: "The machine heard 'is it' twenty-eight times. The song says
  'easy now.' Which one do we cut to?"
- UNC: "We cut to the mouth. The mouth is the record."
- LAURA: "The mouth sang a line that isn't in the song. Whose record is
  that?"
- UNC: "File it under the singer. The singer is always the record."

Narrator test: no line could be spoken over the frame by a narrator. Final
line lands one true message: the crew has agreed to pretend the machine is
fine, and the pretending is now procedure. (Poached move: deflection as
answer — UNC files the problem away instead of answering it.)

### Chain M1-2 — "The Wrong Singer" (DEVELOP)
Stake: PETER's sale — the advertiser paid for a singer, and the singer keeps
changing faces mid-line.
Setting: the control room; the cat on the monitor, the girl's mouth moving.

- PETER: "The advertiser bought a cat, Laura. Sell me back the cat."
- LAURA: "The cat sang it at 12. The girl sang it at 15. Which one did you
  sell?"
- PETER: "I sold the one with the collar. Collars don't change singers."
- COLONEL: "Then announce the collar, Peter. The collar is the singer now."

Narrator test: passes. Final line lands: the official version is whatever
keeps the sale alive. (Poached move: status negotiation — PETER drops from
seller to beggar in two lines; COLONEL rescues him by redefining the product.)

### Chain M1-3 — "A Take Nobody Fired" (DEVELOP → next question)
Stake: the roster itself — a take with no volley behind it means someone, or
something, is firing without UNC.
Setting: the cutting room, late. LAURA holds a finished take file.

- LAURA: "This take has no volley, UNC. No one fired it. Who sings it?"
- UNC: "Check the roster. If it's not on the roster, it didn't happen."
- LAURA: "It IS on the roster. Your handwriting. Dated tomorrow."
- UNC: "Then we shoot it tomorrow. The roster is never wrong."

Narrator test: passes. Final line lands: UNC would rather obey a roster he
did not write than admit the machine writes it now. Cliff opens the next
question: who wrote tomorrow's entry?

## The viral bet

**The wrong-singer flip.** A 10-second clip where the singer changes
mid-line — cat to girl, same word, same pitch, impossible mouth — posted as
a "spot the change" moment. It is a dance of mouths: the handoff itself is
the hook. Secondary bet: **the dropped-word chorus** — the hook the machine
cannot hear, carried by picture alone, as a sing-along the audience has to
complete.

## What it inherits from STATION vs what it breaks

- INHERITS: the cast and their voices; the dialogue-first chain grammar; the
  lore rule (shown, never explained); the roster, the count, the one viewer
  and one advertiser as background pressure; the MV discipline (word-sync,
  tempo gospel, one 16:9 master).
- BREAKS: STATION's engine (promise → failure → funnier failure) is replaced
  by a slow dread engine (fix → fix does not hold → the machine learns); the
  station is no longer the subject — the machine inside it is; the sitcom
  register gives way to workplace uncanny; episodes no longer need a joke —
  they need a wrongness that survives the cut.
