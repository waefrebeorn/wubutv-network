### Slate constraints (research top-10, adopted 2026-11-24)

From the gutting research wave
(`research_notes/gutting-research-20261124/RESEARCH.md` — 45 sources, four
fronts). These bind every show on the slate:

1. **Stand-alone episode + bigger open loop.** Every episode resolves its own
   question on screen, then ends on a bigger unanswered one. Never split one
   story across two posts (the Part-2 tax).
2. **The button is paid in the next cold open, never recapped.** Cut before
   the consequence lands, in the last 3 seconds; the answer is the next
   episode's cold open, paid in its first 10 seconds.
3. **Hook in 3 seconds, in medias res.** Frame 0 is conflict already moving; a
   freeze-frame at second 3 must be understandable with zero backstory.
4. **One turn per episode, a peak every ~30 seconds.** Hook by 3s, reversal by
   ~25–30s, spike before the exit; a 60-second episode carries exactly one
   idea.
5. **Premise conflict is structural.** A power gap (invader holds the status,
   home character holds the secret) generates friction before line one; no
   episode manufactures its conflict from scratch.
6. **Small fixed cast, named and numbered.** 4–6 recurring characters per
   show, each with one visual signature, one chantable name, one signature
   line; episode numbers on screen. The cast is the attachment engine.
7. **Stolen formats need no teaching.** Parody a proven TV engine (dating
   show, sermon, contest, vlog) so the rules need zero explanation — the
   format is the recap.
8. **Cadence is a contract.** Slate-wide floor of 3–5 posts per week; arc
   episodes never more than 48 hours apart; daily during an arc launch.
   Regularity over months beats bursts.
9. **The audience moves the plot.** Where a show has a vote or a
   comment-chosen outcome, it is real: votes convert viewers into
   collaborators.
10. **The one-sentence retell test.** Clarity beats intrigue: every line is
    an action (demand, refuse, lie, reveal); if a cold viewer can't retell
    the episode in one sentence, the episode fails and is cut.

### Law exceptions (parent rulings, 2026-11-24)

## §1 — GATE E, PER PREMISE

Test (Addendum headnote + Method A3): from the one-sentence premise
alone, a cold viewer can say who wants what, what blocks them, and
what changes — **without using a single lore term.**

- **ON AIR — PASS.** Crew wants the true video on air; the station's
  machine keeps slipping in videos nobody made; the slot airs
  something either way. Deadline, job, saboteur — all plain words.
  One clause is imprecise rather than lore-bound: *"sung by a singer
  who doesn't exist"* — the singer visibly exists on screen (a cat);
  what doesn't exist is a *hired, credited* singer, which is also
  what the billing-book plot is about. Fix F1.1 below.
- **HOLD ON — PASS, the slate's closest call.** *"The world restarts
  every ten seconds"* is a world-rule, but it is stated in plain
  English, not a coined lore term (the bible has killed *cull*,
  *tally*, *mark* outright), and it is visible in every unit — bell,
  grab, one detail changed. A cold viewer's restatement ("what
  you're holding when the bell rings is the only proof") uses no
  lore vocabulary. It is the only premise that asks the viewer to
  learn a rule; the bible pays for that in U1, where the grab and
  the bell teach it before the first line lands. Accepted.
- **THE NIGHT LIST — PASS, cleanest on the slate with ONE SHORT.**
  Clerk, list, beds, somebody begging. Nothing to learn.
- **ONE SHORT — PASS, cleanest on the slate with THE NIGHT LIST.**
  A shop, a counter, a count that is always one short. The mystery
  is an arithmetic fact a child can state.
- **THE LAST SEAT — PASS.** Dancer wants the theatre to live; a
  stranger in the critic's seat decides; the landlord is behind
  him. *"A ticket stub that hasn't been printed in twenty years"*
  is a mystery hook in plain words, not a lore term.

No premise fails Gate E. No premise fix in this pass adds
explanation; the one wording fix (F1.1) cuts toward plainness.

## §2 — DISTINCTNESS: THE SLATE'S REAL PROBLEM

**The pair: HOLD ON × THE NIGHT LIST.** Strip the wallpaper and
both shows are the same silhouette:

- a **keeper of a written record** (the Watcher and his chalk wall /
  the Keeper and his list-book) whose writing decides what is true;
- a **rotating guest** who walks up and states a want *about that
  record* (write this / strike that);
- a **questioning second** (Laura in both shows, in the same
  notice-by-U2 / name-by-U4 / act-in-U6 job);
- an **authority reveal read off the record itself** (a line already
  on the wall / a line in the log);
- the same B1 chain, beat for beat: proof → lie → entrance →
  record-reveal → consequence begun.

Cast shape, setting shape (a fixed service point the public comes
to), and episode engine all converge. On a feed, a viewer who has
seen one will read the other as the same show at a different hour.
Grammar-constant is the house doctrine (B13) — but the doctrine
holds grammar constant *within* a show. Across a slate, the
**surface verb** must differ, and these two share theirs: *petition
the keeper to change the writing.*

**Ruling: HOLD ON keeps the shape; THE NIGHT LIST moves.** HOLD
ON's record is inseparable from its proven, physical mechanic —
the held object crossing the reset is the show, and the wall is
only where its verdict lands. THE NIGHT LIST's real stakes were
never the pen: they are **the bed and the doorway** — who sleeps
inside, who sleeps in the rain. Its bible already knows this (E1
ends on *"eleven names and ten beds"*). The fix re-centers the
staging on the physical shortage and demotes the list to
scoreboard. Exact edits: F3.2–F3.4 below.

**Secondary rhymes, noted and accepted with one edit:**

- **Three counters.** HOLD ON (wall), THE NIGHT LIST (desk),
  ONE SHORT (counter) all stage a keeper behind a surface with a
  book or board. ONE SHORT is excused: its verb is **COUNT**, not
  *petition* — the customer brings a load, not a request, and the
  viewer counts along. After the NIGHT LIST fix, the three read as
  street-game / night-allocation / shop-arithmetic. Accepted.
- **Twin door openings.** THE NIGHT LIST's U1 (name struck *as the
  door bangs open*) and ONE SHORT's U1 (door slam + bell + crash)
  are the same opening shape: door-impact + arrival + want-line.
  ONE SHORT's slam is load-bearing — it is the engineered fix for
  its E2 BLAM A1 failure, three sound events stacked in 0.5s — so
  ONE SHORT keeps it. THE NIGHT LIST's impact is the *strike*, not
  the door; its door-bang is redundant and is cut (F3.4).
- **Openings otherwise distinct:** monitor-already-wrong (ON AIR),
  the snatch (HOLD ON), the leap at the seat (THE LAST SEAT). Good
  spread.

## §3 — ENGAGEMENT AUDIT AND RANKING

Per show: the strongest cold-viewer hook, and the exact moment a
viewer most likely swipes.

**1. HOLD ON.** *Hook:* a game with one rule a child can play —
what is in the closed fist when the bell rings? — on the lineage of
the slate's only BLAM-passing pilot and its pacing-praised E2.
*Swipe point:* U3's quiet twist, if the one-detail reset reads as
"nothing changed" instead of a hunt-the-difference beat; the
episode lives or dies on the reset being *visible*.

**2. ONE SHORT.** *Hook:* the only participatory mechanic on the
slate — the viewer counts along and catches the missing one before
the cast does, plus a shelf that fills by one item per episode and
rewards returners. Highest ceiling of the five. *Swipe point:*
U3's second counting batch — if batch two reads as "more counting"
rather than a turn, the middle sags; the accusation must land
*inside* the batch, not after it.

**3. THE LAST SEAT.** *Hook:* the slate's only spectacle — a dancer
mid-leap at a shadowed stranger in frame 0, and a face the season
withholds. *Swipe point:* the U5–U6 back half — a quiet proposal
and a wordless-ish dance completion is the slowest five seconds on
the slate; a viewer who hasn't bought dance-as-verdict leaves
there.

**4. ON AIR.** *Hook:* a deadline that fires whether you're ready
or not, and the wrong-singer flip — plus the slate's only embedded
payoff, a real music-video segment inside every episode. *Swipe
point:* U3 itself, for half the audience — a viewer who came for
the argument gets 10 seconds of music video with 8 words; a viewer
who came for the video gets 50 seconds of office talk around it.
The segment is the show's glory and its leak.

**5. THE NIGHT LIST.** *Hook:* the rawest stakes on the slate —
sleep inside or sleep in the rain, in winter — and the cleanest
premise sentence. Ranked last **as a slate entry**, for two
reasons: until the §2 fix lands, it competes with its own sibling
for the same silhouette; and its E1 reveal is the slate's least
physical — *"the strike was logged at noon"* is a fact a viewer
must **hear**, where every other show's reveal can be **seen**
(a fist opening, a shelf, a credit, a foot tapping). B9's test —
understood from the picture alone — is the one its premiere
outline only passes by dialogue. *Swipe point:* U3, the log
reveal, if the timestamp doesn't land as a picture.

## §5 — PRODUCTION REALITY FLAGS (flagged, not redesigned)

Known generator behaviors applied: speech front-loading,
silence-beat compression, the two-image fire cap, the wordmark law,
and this pipeline's demonstrated unreliability at rendering
*specific legible text* inside a generated take (the wordmark
law's whole history: glyph-exact only when small, shadowed, at a
frame edge).

- **ON AIR — the U1 composite and the U4 credit.** Frame 0 asks for
  a cat singing *with UNC's mouth*: the proven flip is a singer
  *swap*; a cross-species mouth composite is a harder ask and may
  not land clean in the BLAM window. And U4's proof object — the
  on-screen credit **SINGER: UNC** *inside the aired video* — is
  legible text the episode's peak turns on. The credit, like the
  Broadcast Strip, should be treated as a Method C post graphic,
  not a generation request. Also: U5 puts three faces plus the
  billing book in frame; with the two-image anchor cap, the bible
  should declare per unit which two faces anchor (CHAR + PEAK) and
  let the third ride.
- **HOLD ON — the one-detail reset.** The joint grammar asks the
  generator for near-duplicate worlds differing in *exactly one*
  detail (a sign, a coat, a position). Generators change many
  details or none; "one detail different" is a precision ask that
  will sometimes arrive as five details or zero, and the
  hunt-the-difference beat fails silently when it does. Expect a
  higher refire rate on joint units than this show's E2 carry
  discipline suggests. Chalk docket lines on the wall are legible
  text — same post/anchor treatment as above.
- **THE NIGHT LIST — the show *is* handwriting.** Its core device
  is specific names, written and struck, in a book and on a board:
  the strike landing on SOL at frame 0, KEEPER at the bottom of an
  eleven-name board, a log page with a timestamp. None of that can
  be asked of the generator as in-take text. The board and book
  must be produced as generated *blank* surfaces with the writing
  composited in post (the Split Total precedent: device graphics
  are post, keyed to measured beats), or the name-handoff joint
  does not exist. This is the single largest production gap between
  a bible and the pipeline on the slate. (Its five-voice outline
  is a writing fix — F3.1 — not listed here.)
- **ONE SHORT — exact physical counts.** The show's promise is
  that a cold viewer can *count along and catch the missing one
  themselves*. The board's numbers are post graphics and exact —
  proven. The **piles are not**: no generator will reliably render
  exactly 31 umbrellas, then exactly 20 in a batch, countable in
  frame. The count-along bet survives only if the piles read as
  honest piles and the *board* carries the arithmetic — which
  quietly demotes the show's central promise from "count with us"
  to "watch the board." Flagged as this show's defining production
  risk; the room should know the promise is currently written
  stronger than the pipeline can cash. Second flag: the shelf —
  one specific item from each load, present unremarked in the CCTV
  angle, accumulating across episodes — is a precision-placement
  and cross-episode continuity ask on separately generated takes;
  it will need dedicated shelf-angle PEAK panels per episode, and
  U6's shelf-count beat needs those items legible at phone size.
- **THE LAST SEAT — the rhythm tell.** U6's turn is the Occupant's
  foot tapping the *exact rhythm* of the Dancer's phrase and her
  hand conducting one bar from memory — a fine sync ask across a
  cut; expect approximation, and the beat should be staged so an
  approximate tap still reads (it is a tell, not a measurement).
  Everything else in this bible is unusually production-honest:
  the shadowed, identifier-anchored Occupant dodges the
  rotating-face problem by design, the danced-pair grammar is
  E2-proven, and the lines are explicitly written to land wherever
  front-loading puts them.

General note: every bible inherits the standing defenses
(silence-beat compression is answered by A2's placed carriers in
all five outlines — each gap has a named carrier, none is a bare
pause; front-loading is answered by lines written to read wherever
they land, most explicitly in THE LAST SEAT and ONE SHORT, whose
want-line is scripted *into* its impact).

## §6 — NETWORK IDENTITY

**SUPERSEDED 2026-11-24:** the shared skeleton this section celebrates —
one honest shape told five ways — is the disease the gutting cut. The
network's identity is now six different engines under one set of
constraints (see THE GUTTING section above). This section stands as the
record of the pre-gutting identity.

These five shows are one network because they are all the same
honest shape told five ways: a small room where one person holds
the thing everybody else needs — a broadcast slot, a chalk wall, a
list of beds, a count that won't come out even, a single theatre
seat — and every episode, a stranger walks in with a want they say
out loud, a lie they're telling, and one object or piece of paper
that proves the truth, while the person who keeps the room slowly
notices the room itself is not what they thought it was. Nothing
is explained, everything is accused; the talk stops three times an
episode so the viewer can feel it land; and every episode ends
mid-consequence, on a question the viewer can type. It is deadpan,
dense, sixty seconds at a time — five small rooms, five different
games, one promise: you will always know who wants what, and you
will never be told how it ends.
