## 1. SHOW IDENTITY

**Title: THE SHOP**

**Premise (one sentence, Gate E on its face):** *Two neighbours are feuding,
and everything either of them uses to win comes from the same little shop —
where the smiling shopkeeper sells to both sides, and the price of
everything goes up every time somebody loses.*

**The engine, in one breath:** *two rivals escalate a feud by buying each
other's weapons from the same smiling shopkeeper, and the price list is the
season.*

A cold viewer needs nothing else: there is a feud, a shop, a price list,
and a hand reaching for the next weapon. No dialogue teaches any of it; the
picture is the whole syllabus. This is the slate's only wordless show and
its anti-pole: the only one whose escalation is structural (prices rise)
rather than written.

## 2. CAST

Three figures, all wordless. Nobody speaks; nobody needs to.

- **RIVAL ONE — the home character.** A WuBu home character (cast from the
  home bench at the first room; the casting is a parent call). *Want, in
  plain terms:* to still be standing in their own street at the season's
  end. Signature: one visual marker + one repeated gesture the audience can
  chant without words — the roster economics of the slate constraints: one
  visual signature, one chantable name, one signature move.
- **RIVAL TWO — the invader.** A fruit / brainrot / game character, rendered
  as it renders (maximum iconographic casting stands). *Want:* the street,
  the corner, the last of everything — taken one purchase at a time. The
  invader is winning the season on points; that is the lore, shown in the
  price list and the shop's shelves, never explained.
- **THE VENDOR — the celebrity shopkeeper.** The Musk cast pack (already
  built) is the standing vendor likeness. He is physically present in the
  shop, silent, and only ever transacts — he sells to both sides with the
  same smile, and his till is the feud's true scoreboard. (Contrast, per
  the gutting map: ON AIR's Anchor never leaves the screens and only
  speaks; the Vendor never speaks and only transacts.) Likeness law and the
  BUFF PASS apply in full: the face stays faithful to the cast pack;
  exaggeration lives in the body and the shop, never in the face.

Rotating walk-ins (a witness, a delivery creature, next week's victim) may
appear silently as the feud's furniture. Nobody new speaks, because nobody
speaks.

## 3. THE STANDING SHOP SET

One set, built once, amortised across the season (the Boss Bee series-asset
lesson): a small bright shop on the rivals' street — counter, shelves of
boxed upgrades, a till, and **the price list on the wall behind the
counter**, big enough to read at phone size. The street outside the shop
window is the feud's arena; the shop interior is its armoury. The set does
not swap, drift, or get re-dressed between episodes: recognition is the
asset. Upgrades sold in earlier episodes stay visible — on the rivals, on
the street, on the shelves' empty hooks — so the world accumulates (the
process-chain lesson, BT-A2: each round's purchase is the next round's
starting condition).

**Diegetic signage (BT-A3):** the shop's brand lives inside the fiction —
the shop sign, the box labels, and above all the price list are the show's
graphics. Price signage is produced as post layer or in-fiction per BT-A3,
keyed to measured beats; no price is ever a narration.

## 4. THE PRICE-LIST ESCALATION LAW

- **The price list is the season.** Every weapon the feud uses is on the
  list, priced, visible. Prices RISE every round (BT-U1): the same upgrade
  costs more each time it is bought, and the list visibly grows — new lines
  appear as the feud invents new needs.
- **Scarcity is priced, not narrated** (the Night List's salvaged lesson):
  when there is one left, the list says so, and the price says what that
  means.
- **The loser buys.** Each episode's purchase is made by the round's loser,
  buying the winner's last weapon (or its counter) — the arms race (BT-A1)
  is the plot.
- **The till is the scoreboard.** The Vendor's takings climb with the
  prices; the season's real winner is readable off the shop, not off
  either rival.

## 5. EPISODE GRAMMAR

Network format: 60s episodes, 6 units × 10s, 16:9 — wordless pantomime
throughout (see §6 for the budget exception). The grammar is fixed; the
weapon, the crime, and the price rotate.

1. **THE CRIME.** Frame 0 is the feud already moving: one rival's move
   against the other — a theft, a wrecking, a humiliation — at full energy
   in the first second (BLAM law applies unchanged; the impact is
   physical, not spoken).
2. **DISCOVERY BY PROOF.** The victim finds out from a thing, not a teller:
   a held frame, a wall, a witness object (BT-B1's mechanism — the world
   remembers; there is no monitor and no confession).
3. **THE REVENGE.** The victim answers with the last purchase — the arms
   race turns; the revenge overshoots or undershoots in a way the picture
   makes funny or costly.
4. **THE WINNER'S WEAPON, DISPLAYED.** The round's winning move is shown
   working — the process chain (BT-A2) made visible: what it does is the
   spectacle.
5. **THE PURCHASE.** The loser walks into the shop. The Vendor smiles,
   points at the list — the price has risen — and the transaction plays in
   full: money down, box over the counter.
6. **FREEZE ON THE PURCHASE (BT-U2).** The episode freezes on the loser's
   hand touching the next upgrade — the box not yet opened, the price tag
   still on it. That hand is the button: the next episode's cold open pays
   it in its first ten seconds, never recapped (SLATE.md constraints).

Pacing follows the type it rips: short physical beats, median shot ~2.5s,
every beat readable with the sound off and in any language (slate
constraint: meaning survives mute).

## 6. PRODUCTION NOTES

- **Wordless = no word budget.** The A1 episode budget (80–90 words, hard
  cap 95) does NOT bind this show — parent law exception, 2026-11-24,
  recorded in SLATE.md. There are no lines to budget; the dialogue load is
  ~zero by design. All other format law stands (6×10s units, BLAM opening,
  digestion beats carried by picture, the freeze ending per §5.6).
- **The Vendor's likeness** is fired from the Musk cast pack under the
  standing likeness law: faces faithful, exaggeration in the body and the
  shop only (BUFF PASS). The Vendor never sings, never speaks, never
  leaves the shop's counter geometry.
- **Diegetic price signage** (the price list, box labels, the shop sign)
  is post layer or in-fiction per BT-A3 — produced graphics keyed to
  measured beats, never generation requests for legible text inside a
  take (the standing wordmark/text law).
- **The rivals' signatures** (one visual marker + one signature move each)
  are locked at the first room and held constant; new-character drops and
  rivalry escalations are scheduled as content seasons (slate constraint),
  not fired when convenient.
- **Episode numbers on screen** (slate constraint): the price list carries
  the round number — the list is the numbering.

## 7. SEASON SEED

The feud escalates by price, not by script: each round costs more than the
last, the list grows longer, the shop grows richer, and the street grows
poorer — the gentrification war (msg 583) told as a till receipt. The home
rival is losing the season on points, and the audience can read exactly
how much losing costs, because the list is on the wall. The season's turn
is priced in advance: the day the list posts a price neither rival can
pay — and the Vendor, smiling, points at the last line on the list: the
shop itself.
